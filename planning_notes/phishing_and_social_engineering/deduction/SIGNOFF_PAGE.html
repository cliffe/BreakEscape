Who is the agent in the room?

A deduction layer for Break Escape. The brief tells you exactly what marks an ENTROPY agent or a cultist. You gather evidence from people, objects and every puzzle in the building, check each suspect against those rules, and then make calls that have real consequences.

Part 1 · The player's view

One evening at the Institute

Here is fixture A, "Open evening", as a player would play it. It is a test case with placeholder names, but it uses every part of the system.

  1. The brief gives you the rules

    HaX calls before you go in. Five guests are at the Tesseract Institute's open evening. The lodge's people can be recognised three ways, and any one of them is enough:

    Keyholder: carries the brass Tesseract key. There is only one.
    Courier: badged into the archive, wants the Codex, and has no ward charm.
    Devotee: has three or more marked clues.

    HaX also tells you what a wrong call will cost: "Accuse the wrong guest and the lodge closes ranks. People stop talking to you." Nobody says how many guests are guilty. It could be one, all five, or none.

  2. The board shows what you don't know yet

    You open the case file. Each guest has a card with three slots (where they were, what they want, what they carry) and every clue is shown face down. You can see that Marsh carries three things and you know none of them. The profile card counts the clues in play: one brass key, two ward charms, three archive badges.

  3. People talk about other people

    The bar staff and the intern know who went where. The first question is free. Every question after that costs a little goodwill, and if you push too hard the intern goes quiet or, worse, tells someone you've been asking. A kind word wins some of it back. Suspects can show you what's in their bag, but their own word never clears them.

  4. Every puzzle can be evidence

    You pick the cloakroom lock and go through the coats: a ward charm in one pocket, a marked burner SIM in another. You dust Marsh's wine glass and the print shows a sigil ring. The archive badge log clears one guest outright. Each find drops onto the board, marked "New".

  5. A favour, and a risk

    Marsh asks you to fetch something from the cloakroom for her. Doing it shows you everything about her, which is tempting. If she's one of them, you've just run an errand for the lodge. HaX warned you not to do favours for anyone you haven't cleared.

  6. The nudge

    The board says: "You have enough to decide about Venn. Check what's on their card." It never says which way. You look again: everything Venn carries is known, and it's a ward charm. So no key, she can't be a Courier, and she has no marked clues. She's clear.

  7. The one key turns up, and counting pays

    The archive camera shows the brass key in Okafor's hand. There is only one, so nobody else can be a Keyholder, even guests whose pockets you never searched. HaX gives you a choice: confront her now, have it seized after the evening, or watch where she takes it. Confronting her is certain, but it warns the others.

  8. Calls, then the debrief

    You accuse in conversation as you go. When you're ready, HaX asks if you're done ("Not yet" is always an option). Then the debrief goes through each guest: what the evidence said, what you called, and why. For a mistake it shows either "You already held what settled it" or the clue that would have, and where it was.

Why it should be fun

What makes it play well

You're checking rules, not guessing a plot

The profiles are stated up front, so every conclusion is something you can prove. The satisfying moment is "that rules her out", not "I think the author meant him".

Counting is a weapon

Knowing there is only one brass key, or two ward charms, lets you clear people you haven't fully searched. Players feel clever when an elimination lands.

Every room you already know how to play pays off

Lockpicking, PIN safes, dusting, RFID cloning, logs and conversations all feed the same board, so familiar puzzles get a new purpose.

Shortcuts with a price

Pushing a witness, doing a favour or confronting early all save time and all carry risk. The player decides how much to gamble.

The opposition fights back

Evidence gets destroyed at story beats, which rewards collecting early and having a second source. Recovering a shredded note feels like a win.

Calls matter, and you're told how much

Each mission states its stakes. A wrong call can close a route, start a fight or lose the mission, and the debrief shows exactly why, without blame.

Why it teaches

The Cyber Security underneath

The mechanics map onto insider-risk and attribution practice: working from an indicator profile, not a hunch; looking for evidence of absence; needing several indicators rather than one; not stopping at the first match; corroborating before evidence disappears; and remembering that wrongly accusing a colleague does real harm. Pushing a witness and doing favours are social engineering seen from the investigator's side, and the favour that helps a cultist is tailgating with a story.
