## E3 helpdesk reset: learner notes

**Did the brief make sense?** Yes. Kiosk brief ("first you're on the red team ... then you swap seats: you're Leila, taking four calls and one prompt on your own phone. Only Leila's seat is scored") is clear, and the "authorised exercise" scope panel is fine. The weak part is the research stage: "Open the browser and find out what you can about the people involved" does not say that anything not noted is gone for good (E3-2).

**Did the attack-to-defence switch teach something?** Yes, strongly, with the right call. The flip into the queue puts two callers with the same four facts side by side. The feedback after the genuine Gordon says: "This is the real Gordon, giving the same four facts as the earlier caller. The facts couldn't tell them apart; the callback could." That is the memorable line of the episode. Having just talked Leila round by name, team, manager and staff number, the player sees those same four facts fail to identify anyone. The MFA-fatigue step (seven pushes, then "The target tapped Approve ... this is MFA fatigue") then pays off when the queue's last item is an unrequested 02:14 prompt.

**Anything unfair?**
- Choosing Ring back on both genuine callers scores 100 per message but the result screen says "Real mail trusted 0 of 2" (E3-4). The brief says ringing back never costs you, then a stat reads as a miss.
- A learner who skims research can no longer fill the call script and cannot go back (E3-2).
- The two Gordon rows in the inbox are identical ("Gordon McLeish, Claims (calling from a mobile)"), which is the point of the exercise and fair, but the first call's transcript already says "I'll hang up and ring you back on the number we have on file" in the player's own voice (E3-7).

**Was the debrief memorable?** The "On Monday" paragraph is good ("a name, a team, a manager and a staff number can all be found online, so on a phone call they prove nothing ... hang up and ring them back on the number already on file"). The rest of the debrief is a 340-word replay of feedback already read, with raw ids as headings (E3-5), and it never returns to the call the player placed or the MFA attack.

**Confusing UI:** E3-2, E3-4, E3-5, E3-6.

## E5 sign-in pages: learner notes

**Did the brief make sense?** Yes. "put together a fake Quayholt sign-in page from ready-made parts, choose the web address it would sit at, and send one email carrying its link ... Then you swap seats and check five pages" is clear. The Build stage's three pickers (service, address, padlock) are readable and the scoring list is useful.

**Did the attack-to-defence switch teach something?** Yes, and this is the best moment in either episode. The first of the five pages in the defend set is `quayholt-sso.example`, the address the learner chose in the build stage. The feedback names it: "This is quayholt-sso.example, a different site with the words joined by a hyphen, which is also why your password manager offered nothing." The padlock tick teaches "the connection is private to whoever runs that site, not that the site is Quayholt's", which pairs with the builder's own padlock note.

**Anything unfair?** Yes: the research stage is one shot with no way back, and one required piece of evidence is invisible (E5-1, E5-2). Smaller: the "Secrecy" tick on the mailbox page is scored as missed (80 of 100) for "Please use this page rather than ringing the service desk", and the tick list has no urgency option for the same page's "new email will be turned away from 5pm today". That is arguable, not unfair.

**Was the debrief memorable?** "Read the address bar" lands, and closing with "Nobody is marked down for a miss here. Every one of these pages was built to pass for the real thing" is kind. At 559 words it is too long; most of it repeats the per-page feedback.

**Confusing UI:** E5-1, E5-2, E5-3, E5-4, E5-6, E5-7.
