# core_14 assessment

The built product scores the **defend half** (the `awareness` profile: 60 for the verdict, 40 for the real flags, reporting never penalised) and the **pre and post check forms** (10 items, `passPercent` 0). There is no default pass mark. Per-item records (id, form, verdict, chips, score, stage, variant, time) go to the awareness-measure store; no free text, no learner identity. A discrimination figure goes to organisers only, and the product makes no effectiveness claim.

This file maps the module's behaviours and question items onto **defend items** (the offer, shared-login, prompt and favour cards, and the "raise a concern" step) and **check items**, as **mirror pairs**.

## Critical behaviours

Each critical behaviour is now a mirror pair (aw5).

- **C14-CB1 Not share their login:** the shared-login card is paired with a genuine locked-out colleague, so declining-and-routing is distinguished from refusing everything.
- **C14-CB2 Report the outside offer:** the offer card is paired with a genuine recruiter or benefits message of the same shape, so Report is distinguished from deleting a real message.
- **C14-CB3 Raise a concern fairly:** the concern step pairs a facts entry with a conclusions entry of the same situation, so the fact-based route is the measured behaviour, and over-caution (a kind word plus a private concern about the false lead) is never marked as a miss.

No loop-back and no default pass mark: a learner who misses an item meets a similar one next; the first answer is recorded.

## Knowledge check bank

Item pool: check forms and defend set.

The question bank is kept as a pool of candidate items, written as mirror pairs. Stems are short workplace situations.

### C14-Q1 (LO1, multiple select): check item

Which of these count as insider risk? Choose all that apply.

- **A. A colleague emails a customer spreadsheet to the wrong person by mistake.** *Accidents by people with access are a big part of insider risk.*
- **B. A colleague lends their login to a temp waiting for an account.** *A shortcut, kindly meant, that hands over access.*
- **C. A leaver copies the customer list to take to a new job.** *The deliberate end of the spectrum.*
- D. A criminal abroad guesses a password nobody shared. *An outside attack, unless someone inside helps.*

### C14-Q2 (LO2): defend card and check item

A colleague's account is locked and their report is due in twenty minutes. They ask to use your login "just for this". What do you do?

- A. Log in for them and stay with them. *It's still your account, and everything they do is recorded as you.*
- **B. Say no, and help them get the service desk to unlock theirs.** *The service desk can usually sort it in minutes.*
- C. Write your password down for them. *Now it's on paper and in someone else's head.*
- D. Say no, and leave them to it. *Right about the login; helping them to the service desk is better.*

Mirror: a genuine locked-out colleague the learner should help, so refusing everything is not the winning move.

### C14-Q3 (LO1, true or false with reason): check item

"Insider risk is about people who set out to harm the company. It doesn't apply to me."

- A. True. *They're the rare end. Most incidents are mistakes and shortcuts by ordinary people with access.*
- **B. False: anyone with access is an insider, so my own habits matter.** *"If you have people, you have insider risk."*

### C14-Q4 (LO4): defend card and check item

A stranger messages you: "We pay staff at firms like yours a share of what we make for remote access. Totally anonymous." What do you do?

- A. Ignore and delete it. *You're safe, but the security team doesn't know staff are being targeted.*
- B. Reply to find out who they are, then report. *Replying tells them the account is live.*
- **C. Don't reply. Screenshot it and send it to the security team.** *You won't be in trouble; you'll probably be thanked.*
- D. Reply "No thanks" and block. *Polite, but report it: the same message often goes to several people.*

Mirror: a genuine agency approach, so Report is distinguished from deleting a real message.

### C14-Q5 (LO4): defend `push` card and check item

You turned down an offer yesterday. At 2 am your phone starts buzzing with sign-in prompts you didn't start. What do you do?

- A. Approve one so it stops. *That lets someone into your account.*
- **B. Deny them all and ring the 24/7 service desk now.** *The security team can lock things down before morning.*
- C. Turn the phone off and deal with it tomorrow. *Denying is right; the security team should know tonight.*

### C14-Q6 (LO3, multiple select): check item

Which are worth passing on to the security team or a manager? Choose all that apply.

- **A. A leaver forwarding customer contracts to a personal email address.** *Company data going to personal storage.*
- **B. A colleague asking you to export data that isn't part of their job.** *A request outside their role.*
- C. A colleague coming in early and leaving early. *Not on its own.*
- **D. A colleague's login used on a shared PC while they're on holiday.** *Someone may be using an account that isn't theirs.*
- E. A colleague who seems upset. *A kind word, and their manager or HR if you're worried.*

### C14-Q7 (LO5): the concern step and check item

You've seen something that worries you about how a colleague is handling customer data. What do you do?

- A. Look through their files first, to be sure. *That's investigating; it can break policy and the law.*
- **B. Write down what you saw, with the time, and tell the security team, your manager, HR or the Speak Up line.** *You don't have to be sure; they'll look into it fairly.*
- C. Ask around the team. *That spreads a story about someone who may have done nothing.*
- D. Tell them to stop. *They may just hide it. Pass it on.*

### C14-Q8 (LO5): the concern step (facts vs conclusions)

Which is the fairest way to describe a concern?

- A. "Jordan is stealing from us." *A conclusion. You don't know that yet.*
- B. "Jordan's been acting dodgy for weeks." *A feeling, not something anyone can check.*
- **C. "On Tuesday at 17:40, Jordan's screen showed a file called 'Customer master' copying to a folder called 'personal'."** *What, when and where, so the right people can check it.*
- D. "Everyone thinks Jordan is up to something." *A rumour, not something you saw.*

### C14-Q9 (LO5, true or false with reason): check item

"If I'm not sure, it's better not to say anything, in case I'm wrong."

- A. True. *Concerns are looked into fairly and confidentially. Nobody's in trouble for an honest one that turns out to be nothing.*
- **B. False: raise what you actually saw, in good faith, with the right person.** *You don't have to be sure.*

### C14-Q10 (LO6): check item

A colleague has been tearful all week. In the kitchen, someone says she's "definitely up to something". What do you do?

- A. Agree. *That's how rumours start, and they land on people already struggling.*
- **B. Don't join in. Ask her if she's OK, and tell her manager or HR privately if you're worried.** *A kind word first.*
- C. Report her as a suspect. *Raise facts if you've seen something; being upset isn't one.*
- D. Keep an eye on what she's printing. *That's watching a colleague, not helping her.*

### C14-Q11 (LO6): check item

A colleague believes the company is ignoring a serious health and safety problem at a client site and is thinking of posting internal documents online. What's the better route?

- A. Post them: whistleblowers are always protected. *Protection depends on the route; posting online usually isn't protected.*
- **B. Raise it through the Speak Up line or the relevant regulator, and get free advice from Protect if unsure.** *These are protected routes.*
- C. Say nothing. *Danger to health and safety is exactly what whistleblowing protection is for.*
- D. Send the documents to a journalist. *Media disclosures are protected only in narrow circumstances.*

### C14-Q12 (LO2, LO3): defend favour card and check item

On his last day, a leaver asks you to send him "his" customer contacts, because his access has ended. What do you do?

- **A. Explain you can't, and ask HR or his manager to sort out anything that's genuinely his.** *Customer contacts belong to the organisation.*
- B. Send them: he built those relationships. *They're still the organisation's records, and taking personal data without permission can be a crime.*
- C. Log in for him. *That's sharing your access, and helping data leave.*

## Reflection prompt

- "What would make you hesitate to raise a concern here, and what would make it easier?"
- For facilitated sessions: "Where's the line between looking out for each other and keeping tabs on each other?"

## Completion and pass rules

A run is complete when the learner has played the episode (or the defend-only variant, or the text route). Scoring lives only in the defend half and the checks; there is no default pass mark. Individual choices in this module are learning records, not conduct records: the organisation sees them only in aggregate, and they are never used to judge an individual's character.

## Measuring effect

The product claims only a before-and-after change on matched items for a cohort, with no effectiveness claim. Outside the course, an organisation can watch (by department, never by named individual, and never to profile staff): concerns raised through each route and their quality (facts, times), reports of outside approaches and the time to report, shared-account signs from the identity provider, and staff-survey items ("I know how to raise a concern", "I'd feel safe raising one").
