# core_14 concepts: Insider risk

This file is the source of truth for the module. The game, the explainers, the knowledge check and the reference card must all match it.

## Key messages

1. **Insider risk is mostly ordinary.** Anyone with access is an insider, including you. Most incidents come from mistakes and shortcuts by good people; occasionally someone does harm on purpose.
2. **Your login is you.** Never share your password, codes or sign-in prompts, and never use anyone else's. Locked out? The service desk sorts it in minutes.
3. **If anyone offers you money or favours for access or data, don't reply.** Screenshot it and report it to the security team. You won't be in trouble for being asked.
4. **Notice it, note the facts, pass it on.** Tell the security team, your manager, HR, the Speak Up line or the DPO what you saw: what, when and where. Don't investigate, confront or gossip. You don't have to be sure; you do have to be fair.
5. **Unusual isn't the same as wrong.** A colleague having a hard time needs a kind word first. Real wrongdoing has protected routes for raising it; leaking isn't one of them.

## Concepts

### What insider risk is

The UK's National Protective Security Authority (NPSA, formerly CPNI) changed its definitions in May 2023 so that they include harm done without intent. In summary:

- An **insider** is anyone who has, or previously had, authorised access to or knowledge of an organisation's resources (people, processes, information, technology and buildings): employees, contractors, temporary staff, partners.
- **Insider risk** is the likelihood of harm or loss to the organisation, and its impact, because of the action or inaction of an insider, whether intended or not.
- **Insider threat** is the narrower term for an insider who intends to cause harm, or is likely to.
- An **insider event** (the term NPSA introduced in May 2023) is an action or inaction by an insider that causes harm, or has the potential to, whether that insider meant harm or not.

NPSA's 2026 principles for a shared approach to insider risk describe a "spectrum of intent" from unintentional to intentional, note that warning signs are often visible early enough to intervene, and put the message simply: "If you have people, you have insider risk."

| Where on the spectrum | What it looks like at work |
|---|---|
| **Accidental** | An email to the wrong Sam; a laptop left on a train; a spreadsheet shared with "anyone with the link" |
| **Careless** (cutting corners) | Lending a login to a colleague; emailing work to a personal account to finish at home; using an unapproved tool because the approved one is slow |
| **Deliberate** | Taking the customer list to a new job; selling data; letting criminals in for a share of the proceeds; damaging systems out of a grudge |

Industry surveys consistently report more insider incidents caused by negligence and mistakes than by malice (the Ponemon Institute and DTEX "Cost of Insider Risks" reports; check the latest edition's figure before quoting a percentage). The module spends most of its time on the everyday end and on fair, early reporting, which helps at every point on the spectrum.

### Why people cross the line

Deliberate cases rarely come from nowhere. The usual drivers:

- **Grievance:** feeling passed over or badly treated (the Morrisons case below).
- **Money:** debts, or an offer that seems easy (the BBC approach and the Coinbase case).
- **Pressure or coercion:** threats, blackmail, or someone exploiting a debt or a secret.
- **Belief:** being convinced the organisation is in the wrong and that taking or releasing information is justified.
- **Opportunity and a story they tell themselves:** "they're my contacts", "everyone does it", "they owe me".

None of these makes a person a suspect. Most people with debts, grievances or strong views never do anything wrong. Organisations reduce risk by treating people fairly, supporting them when they are struggling, and making it easy to raise concerns early (NPSA's principles stress both culture and early intervention).

### Your login is you

- Anything done with your account is recorded as you: the emails, the files opened, the deletions.
- A shared login breaks the record of who did what, may show the other person things they shouldn't see, and keeps working after they have left.
- Sharing includes telling someone your password, reading out a code, approving a sign-in prompt for them, and logging in for them "just for two minutes".
- Using someone else's login is the same thing from the other side.
- There is always a proper route: the service desk resets a locked account in minutes, and a manager can arrange temporary access.
- Criminals ask for access too, sometimes pretending to be colleagues or IT (core_02 and core_03 cover those calls and prompts).

### Leavers and data

Notice periods are a known moment of risk: people take things they feel they made or might need. The rules are simple:

- **Company information stays with the company:** customer lists, prices, contracts, designs, code, plans. Personal data in them belongs to the people it is about, not to the member of staff who looked after it.
- **Taking personal data without the employer's consent can be a crime.** Under section 170 of the Data Protection Act 2018 it is an offence to knowingly or recklessly obtain, disclose or keep personal data without the consent of the organisation responsible for it, and to sell data obtained that way. The UK regulator prosecutes these cases. (Since 30 September 2026 the regulator is the Information Commission, still known as the ICO; before that it was the Information Commissioner.)
- **What is fine:** your own personal files (photos, payslips, your own contact details), with HR's help; handover notes in the shared place, for your successor.
- **Facts worth noticing:** company data being copied to personal email, personal cloud storage or USB sticks; someone asking others to export or send data outside their role; access to things outside a person's job; getting round controls (photographing screens, switching off tools, using personal devices to move files).
- **Not worth reading anything into:** working late, being upset, visiting HR, printing, handing in a notice.

### Criminals recruiting insiders

Ransomware gangs and fraud groups buy access from staff. They make contact through messaging apps, social networks, fake recruiters or in person, offer a share of the proceeds, and ask for a login, remote access, a script to be run or a device to be plugged in. A refusal can be followed by pressure, such as a flood of sign-in prompts.

- **Don't reply, negotiate or "play along to find out more".**
- **Screenshot the message and report it** to the security team straight away. One report can warn the whole organisation; the same message is often sent to several people.
- **If sign-in prompts arrive that you didn't start, deny them all and report it now,** day or night (core_03).
- **You won't be in trouble for being approached.** If you feel pressured or threatened (a debt, a threat, something being held over you), tell the security team or HR: they can help, and it isn't your fault.
- **States do this too.** NPSA's "Think Before You Link" campaign warns that hostile actors pose as recruiters on professional networking sites; its advice is to recognise the approach, report it to your security manager and remove the contact.

### Raising a concern fairly

- **Notice** something specific you saw or heard yourself.
- **Note the facts** somewhere of your own: what, when, where, who. Not what you think it means.
- **Pass it on to the right person** (see the procedure). If unsure, the security team will route it.
- **Then let them handle it.** Looking into a concern is a job for people with the authority and a fair, lawful process. You may not hear the outcome, because that is confidential too.
- **Don't investigate.** Searching someone's desk, reading their screen or messages, photographing their files or "testing" them can break policy and the law, can spoil a proper investigation, and is unfair if you're wrong. A photo of someone's screen may itself put customer data on your phone.
- **Don't confront or gossip.** Confronting may warn someone to hide what they're doing; gossip harms people who have done nothing, and rumours tend to land on people who are already struggling.
- **Good faith is enough.** Nobody is in trouble for raising an honest concern that turns out to be nothing. Knowingly false accusations are a different matter.
- NPSA's "It's OK to Say" programme was built because its research found staff under-reported concerning behaviour. It notes that such behaviour can be an early sign of insider activity or of a welfare problem, and both are better caught early.

### Unusual isn't wrong: supporting colleagues

- People have hard weeks: illness, caring for someone, bereavement, money worries, a relationship ending, a grievance at work. It shows as arriving early or late, private calls, visits to HR, distraction, tears.
- **The right first response is human:** a kind word, a cup of tea, "I'm around if you want to talk". Point them to the employee assistance programme if there is one. If you're worried about their wellbeing, tell their manager or HR privately.
- **Support is also good security.** People under pressure are the people criminals target, and people who feel supported are less likely to be pushed into a bad decision.
- **Rumours are not evidence.** Don't start them, add to them or pass them on. If someone shares a theory with you, a fair answer is "If you've actually seen something, tell the security team or your manager. Not the kitchen."

### Whistleblowing or leaking

In the UK, the Public Interest Disclosure Act 1998 added protections for whistleblowers to the Employment Rights Act 1996 (Part IVA). In outline (not legal advice):

- A **protected disclosure** is information that the worker reasonably believes is in the public interest and tends to show one or more of: a criminal offence; a failure to meet a legal obligation; a miscarriage of justice; danger to someone's health or safety; sexual harassment (added from 6 April 2026 by the Employment Rights Act 2025); damage to the environment; or a deliberate cover-up of any of these (Employment Rights Act 1996, section 43B).
- It must be made through a protected route: to the employer (including a Speak Up line), to a "prescribed person" (a regulator listed in law, such as the ICO for data protection), or to a legal adviser. Wider disclosures, for example to the media, are protected only in narrow circumstances.
- Workers who blow the whistle this way are protected from being dismissed or treated badly for it, and a confidentiality clause cannot stop a protected disclosure.
- **Personal grievances are not whistleblowing** unless they are in the public interest. They go through the grievance procedure.
- **Leaking is different.** Copying confidential or personal data and handing it to a competitor, a journalist or the internet is not the protected route, and it can be a crime under section 170 of the Data Protection Act 2018 as well as a breach of contract. That section has defences (for example where the conduct was justified in the public interest), but they have to be proved afterwards; the protected routes don't carry that risk. Calling yourself a whistleblower after taking a customer list doesn't make you one.
- **Free, confidential advice:** Protect, the UK whistleblowing charity, runs an advice line for workers.
- **Elsewhere:** staff in the EU are covered by national laws under the EU Whistleblower Directive (Directive (EU) 2019/1937). Organisations outside the UK should localise this section.

## What to do (the procedure)

As the reference card shows it:

**Your login is you**

1. Never share your password, a code or a sign-in prompt, and never log in for anyone. Never use someone else's account.
2. Locked out, or a colleague is? Call the service desk.

**Offers and pressure**

3. Someone offers money or favours for access or data? Don't reply. Screenshot it and report it to the security team.
4. Sign-in prompts you didn't start? Deny them all and call the service desk now, any hour.
5. Feeling pressured or threatened? Tell the security team or HR. You won't be in trouble.

**Concerns about how data or access is used**

6. Notice: something you saw or heard yourself.
7. Note the facts: what, when, where, who.
8. Pass it on to the right person:

| What it's about | Who to tell |
|---|---|
| Data or access being misused, outside approaches, anything security | The security team |
| Conduct or a colleague's wellbeing | Your manager or HR |
| Wrongdoing you'd rather raise anonymously, or that your manager is part of | The Speak Up line |
| Personal data | The DPO (or the security team, who will pass it on) |

9. Then let them look into it. Don't investigate, confront or gossip.

**Colleagues**

10. A kind word first. Worried about someone's wellbeing? Their manager or HR, privately.

## Misconceptions and outdated advice

| Belief | The current position |
|---|---|
| "Insider threats are spies and saboteurs." | Mostly they are mistakes and shortcuts by ordinary staff. Deliberate cases are rare, but they are often visible early to colleagues. |
| "Lending my login to someone I trust is fine." | Everything they do is recorded as you, and they may see more than they should. The service desk can sort access in minutes. |
| "If I watch them while they use my login, it's fine." | It is still your account and your record, and you can't see everything they open. |
| "My clients are my clients." | Customer lists and contacts belong to the organisation, and the personal data in them to the people themselves. Taking them can be a crime. |
| "If I'm approached by criminals, I'll be under suspicion." | Reporting an approach is exactly what the security team wants. People who report are thanked, as the BBC journalist was. |
| "I should gather evidence before I say anything." | Note what you saw. Don't search, photograph or test: it can be unlawful, can spoil an investigation, and is unfair if you're wrong. |
| "I need to be sure before I raise it." | You don't. Raise facts in good faith and let the right people decide. |
| "Raising a concern about a colleague is grassing." | A private, fair concern to the right person can stop harm to customers and colleagues, and often gets the person help early. Gossip is what hurts people. |
| "Odd behaviour means something's going on." | Usually it means someone's having a hard time. A kind word first. |
| "Anyone who leaks wrongdoing is a protected whistleblower." | Protection depends on what is disclosed, a reasonable belief that it's in the public interest, and the route used. Leaking data to a competitor or online usually isn't protected. |

## Real cases

Use these as background and inspiration only. Game content uses fictional names.

- **BBC journalist approached by a ransomware group, July 2025.** The BBC's cyber correspondent Joe Tidy was contacted on the Signal messaging app by someone calling themselves "Syndicate", who claimed to represent the Medusa ransomware gang. He was offered 15% of any ransom for access to BBC systems, with the offer later raised. When he stalled, his phone was flooded with sign-in prompts. He reported it to the BBC's information security team, who disconnected him from BBC systems as a precaution; the attempt failed. (BBC News, September 2025; BleepingComputer, "Ransomware gang sought BBC reporter's help in hacking media giant", 29 September 2025.)
- **Coinbase, May 2025.** The cryptocurrency exchange said criminals had paid a small number of overseas customer support staff and contractors to copy customer data from its support tools, then demanded US$20 million. Coinbase refused, offered a US$20 million reward for information instead, and estimated costs of US$180 to 400 million in its filing to the US Securities and Exchange Commission. (CNBC and NBC News, 15 May 2025.)
- **Car leasing salesman, prosecuted 2024.** A sales consultant at a Hertfordshire car leasing company copied more than 3,600 customer records shortly before resigning and offered them to rival firms, claiming they were his own. He pleaded guilty under section 170 of the Data Protection Act 2018 and was fined. (ICO, "Guilty car salesman fined for retaining and selling data to competitors", September 2024.)
- **Morrisons, 2014 (Supreme Court 2020).** A senior internal auditor, aggrieved after a disciplinary warning, copied payroll data he had been given for the external auditors and posted the details of almost 100,000 colleagues online, using a co-worker's identity to try to frame him. He was jailed. The Supreme Court later ruled that Morrisons was not vicariously liable for his actions. (*WM Morrison Supermarkets plc v Various Claimants* [2020] UKSC 12.)

## Organisation-specific slots

| Slot | Default (example) |
|---|---|
| Security team contact | The security team, or the Report button for messages |
| Service desk | The 24/7 service desk line |
| HR contact | Your HR Business Partner |
| Speak Up line | A "Speak Up" line run by an independent provider, by phone or web, anonymous if you prefer |
| DPO | The organisation's Data Protection Officer |
| Whistleblowing policy | The organisation's "Speaking Up" policy on the intranet |
| Regulator most relevant to the organisation | The ICO for data protection; others by sector |
| Leaver process | Access ends at 12:00 on the last day; HR helps leavers collect personal files |
| Personal email and cloud | Not to be used for the organisation's information |
| Wellbeing support | The employee assistance programme, 24 hours, confidential |
| Monitoring notice | "Our systems log access to customer data and large file transfers. Here's what we monitor and why." |
| Outside approaches | Screenshot and send to the security team's address, or press Report |

## Glossary

- **Insider:** anyone who has, or had, authorised access to an organisation's people, information, systems or buildings.
- **Insider risk:** the chance of harm because of something an insider does or doesn't do, on purpose or not.
- **Insider threat:** an insider who intends to cause harm, or is likely to.
- **Credentials:** what proves who you are to a system: a username, a password, a code, a passkey.
- **Notice period:** the time between someone resigning and leaving.
- **Leaver process:** the steps that end a leaver's access and recover devices and data (role_05 covers it for managers).
- **Speak Up line:** a confidential, often anonymous, way to raise concerns about wrongdoing.
- **Whistleblowing:** raising concerns about wrongdoing in the public interest through a protected route.
- **Protected disclosure:** a disclosure that qualifies for whistleblowing protection under UK law.
- **Prescribed person:** a regulator or body listed in law to receive whistleblowing disclosures.
- **Leaking:** releasing confidential information outside the protected routes.
- **Prompt storm (MFA fatigue):** a flood of sign-in approval requests sent to wear someone down (core_03).
