# core_17 concepts: Capstone: the annual assessment

This file is the source of truth for the capstone's own messages: chains, busy days, the year's changes, the profile and the annual cycle. **It teaches no topic in depth.** For every threat in the day, the core module that teaches it is the source of truth (`00_COURSE_OVERVIEW.md`, topic boundaries), and the capstone's moments, debrief lines and check items must match that module's `concepts.md`. Each edition's "what changed this year" section is rewritten and re-checked before release.

## Key messages

1. **Attacks come as chains of small moments,** often across channels: a text, then a call, then an email. Break the chain at the first one: pause, check another way, report.
2. **The habit works on every channel.** When a message pressures you to move money, grant access, sign in or read back a code, stop and check it yourself through a route you already trust, not the link, number or page it gives you. An email, a call, a voice note, a visitor, a pop-up, a favour from a colleague: the same step.
3. **Busy days are when it matters most.** Year end, holiday cover, the afternoon before a long weekend: attackers like them, and the procedures exist for exactly those days. No shortcut is worth it.
4. **Report your own slip straight away.** The sooner, the smaller. It's what the course rewards most.
5. **Pass it on.** Help the newer person next to you do the same, without blame. That's how a team stays safe.

## Concepts

### What the capstone is for

- **Assessment of habits.** The day puts most of the year's threats into one realistic shift, unannounced, inside ordinary work. What the learner does is the evidence (stealth assessment: Shute, 2011; evidence-centred design: Mislevy, Almond and Lukas, 2003). The knowledge check adds evidence everyone recognises.
- **A short refresh,** not a lecture: three minutes on what changed this year, then practice.
- **A router to practice.** Each learner leaves with a private year profile and up to two spotlights for the year ahead, delivered as modules and monthly drills.
- **What it is not:** a re-run of every module, a test of trivia, or a way to rank staff.

### Why once a year isn't enough on its own

- **Skills fade.** In a field study of 409 employees at a German public administration, people's improved ability to tell phishing from genuine email held at four months after training but had gone by six; video and interactive reminders helped (Reinheimer and others, 2020).
- **Annual training alone shows little effect.** In an eight-month randomised study of more than 19,500 employees at a US healthcare organisation, there was no significant relationship between whether people had recently completed annual awareness training and their likelihood of failing a phishing simulation (Ho and others, 2025).
- **Reporting does work at scale.** In a 15-month study of more than 14,000 employees, staff reports detected new phishing campaigns quickly and reliably (Lain, Kostiainen and Čapkun, 2022).
- **So the course is built for spacing and practice:** monthly micro-drills between capstones (spaced practice: Cepeda and others, 2006), a check that is itself retrieval practice (Roediger and Karpicke, 2006), and a capstone that measures habits and points to the next practice. None of this replaces technical controls such as MFA and filtering; it is the human half of the defence.

### Chains: how one small moment leads to the next

A chain is a sequence in which each step uses what the last one gained. Seen one at a time, each step looks small: an MFA prompt approved to make it stop, a contact number shared, a supplier's "new bank details" passed on. Seen together, they are an attack.

- **Retool, 2023.** Text messages sent staff to a fake sign-in page; one person entered their details. A caller then used a deepfaked voice of a member of the IT team to get an MFA code, and the attacker took over the accounts of 27 cloud customers.
- **Marks and Spencer, 2025.** Impersonation involving a third party got the attackers in; months of disruption followed (core_00's concepts).
- **British Library, 2023.** Compromised credentials on a remote access server without MFA were the most likely way in, and the attackers were inside for at least three days before anyone knew (core_00's concepts).

**Where a chain breaks:**

| Link | What breaks it | Taught in |
|---|---|---|
| A sign-in prompt you didn't start | Deny it and report it | core_03 |
| A message that wants you to sign in, pay or share | Don't use the link; check another way; report | core_01, core_02 |
| A caller who wants a code, remote access or a change | Hang up; ring back on a number you already have | core_02 |
| A change of bank details | Call back on the number on record; pass it to Finance without vouching for it | core_11 |
| A person at the door | Ask who they're here to see; walk them to reception | core_10 |
| A file sent to the wrong place | Report it at once, so it can be recalled or deleted | core_04, core_05, core_06 |
| Any of the above, after the fact | Report it now: the sooner, the fewer links follow | core_04 |

The earliest report breaks the most links. In the game, every chain can be broken at any point by reporting, and the debrief shows the difference the timing made.

### Busy days and pressure

- **Attackers like busy, quiet or distracted times.** The FBI and the US Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency warned in 2021 that they had "observed an increase in highly impactful ransomware attacks occurring on holidays and weekends", when offices are closed and IT teams are thin (advisory AA21-243A). Fraudsters lean on the same moments: year-end deadlines, holiday cover, the afternoon before a long weekend (core_11).
- **Pressure is the tell.** "Before the cut-off", "before the weekend", "the chief executive has agreed it": urgency plus money, codes, data or access is the pattern to pause on (core_01, core_11).
- **Procedures exist for busy days.** Call-backs, two people approving, visitors escorted: these feel slowest when time is shortest, which is exactly why they're there. Keeping to them under pressure, including from senior names, is a skill the capstone assesses (C17-LO3).

### This year's picture (Edition 2027)

Rewritten for each edition from that year's sources, and re-checked before release. The 2027 draft, from what was published by October 2026:

- **Phishing is still the most common attack, and more often the only one.** In the Cyber Security Breaches Survey 2025/2026, phishing remained the most prevalent type of breach or attack by far (38% of businesses, 25% of charities); among organisations that had a breach or attack, the share whose only one was phishing rose to 51% of businesses and 57% of charities.
- **Serious incidents are rising.** The NCSC's Annual Review 2025 recorded 204 nationally significant incidents in the year to August 2025, up from 89, with ransomware reported most by academia, finance, engineering, retail, health and manufacturing.
- **Suppliers and stolen logins are common ways in.** Verizon's 2025 Data Breach Investigations Report found third parties involved in 30% of breaches (double the year before), and credential abuse (22%) the leading way in.
- **Impersonation over the phone and by voice.** The 2025 retail attacks reportedly began with calls impersonating staff (core_02, role_03), and voice cloning means a familiar voice is no longer proof (core_15).

The proctor's three headline changes for the 2027 brief (spoken lines are generic; any figures are on screen only):

1. **"Scams switch channels."** A text, then a call, then an email. Check another way, every time.
2. **"A voice isn't proof."** A voice note or call from someone you know can be faked. Call back on the number you already have.
3. **"Your login is the prize."** A prompt you didn't start, a caller who wants a code, a colleague who wants your password: all the same request.

### A fair assessment for everyone

- **Every threat can be handled with the habit.** Someone who hasn't taken core_11 may not know the term "Confirmation of Payee", but they can still pause on a bank detail change, check on the number on record and pass it to Finance. The edition only rates what the habit covers; the detail is for the module.
- **"New to you" areas.** Where the learner hasn't taken the module behind a moment, the profile marks the area "new to you", the moment still counts for the critical behaviours that apply to everyone, and the module is recommended rather than the result held against them.
- **No speed, no tricks, no traps.** Game time is not real time, threats never arrive two at once (BE-18's busy gate), every chain can be broken at any point, and nothing irreversible happens before the debrief.
- **Same evidence by every route:** the game, the text route (BE-03) and the team event's individual check.

### The year profile and spotlights

What learners are told (on the intranet and in the learner home):

- **Your year profile** shows, for each area of the course (one per core module, from email to the rules), how you did in this year's capstone: Secure, Developing, Not yet, new to you, or not assessed this year. Last year's rating sits beside it. (The rules for building it are in `assessment.md`.)
- **Spotlights:** up to two areas to practise this year, with the module or the monthly drill that covers each. They're for you; your manager doesn't see them.
- **Who sees what:** you see your profile; your organisation sees areas only for groups of ten or more, and your completion and check score as for any module. Your choices are practice and never used for discipline or performance reviews (`features/assessment_and_evidence.md`, section 11).

### Helping a newer colleague

The 2027 edition gives the player a new apprentice to shadow them for the day, because by their second year most staff are the person someone else asks. Good help sounds like this:

- **Say what to do, not what they did wrong:** "Let's check that one with Finance first", not "You nearly fell for that."
- **Give the reason in one line:** "A voice can be faked. We ring back on the number we know."
- **Do it with them:** walk them through the Report button or the call-back, rather than taking over.
- **Thank them for asking.** The NCSC's cyber security culture principles stress social norms and openness: people copy what colleagues do, and they report more where asking is welcomed.

### The annual policy re-acknowledgement

PCI DSS 12.6.3 asks staff to acknowledge, at least every 12 months, that they have read and understood the security policy and procedures. The capstone ends with HA-07's re-acknowledgement for any policy due (core_16 explains what acknowledging means). It is offered, not forced into the game: a learner can do it from the learner home instead.

## What to do (the procedure)

The **Year End card**: the whole course on one page, headed with the habit to keep ("When a message pressures you to move money, grant access, sign in or read back a code, stop and check it yourself through a route you already trust, not the link, number or page the message gives you. Taking a minute to confirm is always a fair thing to do.") and the organisation's reporting routes (BE-05). One line per area, each linking to its module's reference card:

1. **Emails:** don't sign in, pay or share through links in unexpected messages. Use the Report button (core_01).
2. **Calls, texts and QR codes:** hang up and call back on a number you already have. Never share a code or allow remote access because someone asked (core_02).
3. **Passwords and MFA:** three random words and a password manager. Approve only the prompts you started (core_03).
4. **Something wrong?** Report it now, including your own slip, with what, when and what you did (core_04).
5. **Personal data:** use only what's needed; requests for someone's data go to the DPO the same day (core_05).
6. **Sending and sharing:** check every recipient; send by the method the label needs (core_06).
7. **Devices:** install updates; never plug in an unknown USB stick; treat a security alert as a reason to report (core_07, core_13).
8. **The web:** software only from the approved source; close any page that tells you to run something (core_08).
9. **Away from the office:** mind your screen and your calls; lost a device? Report it straight away (core_09).
10. **The building:** wear your badge, never lend it, walk visitors to reception, keep a clear desk (core_10).
11. **Money:** check any change of bank details on the number on record, and pass it to Finance (core_11).
12. **Posting online:** check what's in shot, and don't speak for the organisation (core_12).
13. **Tools:** approved ones only; not on the list? Ask first (core_13, core_16).
14. **Access:** never share or borrow a login; report anyone offering money for access (core_14).
15. **AI and voices:** approved AI tools only, with no personal or confidential data in public ones; a voice isn't proof (core_15).

## Misconceptions and outdated advice

| Belief | The current position |
|---|---|
| "The annual course is what keeps us safe." | Annual training alone shows little effect on its own (Ho and others, 2025). Habits, easy reporting, regular short practice and technical controls do the work together. |
| "I passed last year, so I'm fine." | Skills fade within months (Reinheimer and others, 2020). That's why the capstone tests habits and sets practice for the year ahead. |
| "One small slip doesn't matter." | Attacks are chains; a small slip is often the first link. Report it and the chain stops there. |
| "On a busy day, a shortcut is fine just this once." | Busy days are when attackers strike and when procedures matter most (FBI and CISA, 2021). |
| "If the chief executive says so, the process can wait." | The process protects senior people too; that's why fraudsters borrow their names (core_11). |
| "I'd know my boss's voice anywhere." | Voices can be cloned. Check another way (core_15). |
| "The profile will be used against me." | It's private, and choices in the game are never used for discipline (section 11 of the assessment design). |
| "Being fooled by an authorised test means I'm bad at this." | Testers are professionals. Reporting what you noticed is the win, and the test is how the organisation finds what to fix. |

## Glossary

- **Capstone:** the annual module that brings the year's topics together in one day.
- **Edition:** that year's version of the capstone, with new threats and a new "this year" briefing.
- **Chain:** a series of small steps in an attack, each using what the last one gained.
- **Stealth assessment:** judging what someone can do from what they do in the game, rather than from a separate test.
- **Year profile:** a learner's private view of their results by area for the year, with last year's beside it.
- **Spotlight:** a module or drill recommended for the year ahead, chosen from the profile (personal) or by the organisation (for everyone).
- **Micro-drill:** a 3 to 5 minute practice session, sent monthly.
- **Authorised test:** a test of the organisation's defences, by a company it has hired, with written permission (an authorised tester's job).
- **Re-acknowledgement:** confirming again, usually yearly, that you've read and understood a policy.
- **Checkpoint:** a saved point where the module can be paused and resumed later.
