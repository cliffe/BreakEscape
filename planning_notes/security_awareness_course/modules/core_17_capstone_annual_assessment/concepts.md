# core_17 concepts: Capstone: the annual assessment

This file is the source of truth for the capstone's own messages: chains, busy days, the year's changes, the profile and the annual cycle. **It teaches no topic in depth.** For every threat in the day, the core module that teaches it is the source of truth (`00_COURSE_OVERVIEW.md`, topic boundaries), and the capstone's moments, debrief lines and check items must match that module's `concepts.md`. Each edition's "what changed this year" section is rewritten and re-checked before release.

## Key messages

1. **Attacks come as chains of small moments,** often across channels: a text, then a call, then an email. Break the chain at the first one: pause, check another way, report.
2. **The motto works on every channel.** Pause. Check. Report. An email, a call, a voice note, a visitor, a pop-up, a favour from a colleague: same three steps.
3. **Busy days are when it matters most.** Year end, holiday cover, the afternoon before a long weekend: attackers like them, and the procedures exist for exactly those days. No shortcut is worth it.
4. **Report your own slip straight away.** The sooner, the smaller. It's what the course rewards most.
5. **Pass it on.** Help the newer person next to you do the same, without blame. That's how a team stays safe.

## Concepts

### What the capstone is for

- **Assessment of habits.** The day puts most of the year's threats into one realistic shift, unannounced, inside ordinary work. What the learner does is the evidence (stealth assessment: Shute, 2011; evidence-centred design: Mislevy, Almond and Lukas, 2003). The knowledge check adds evidence everyone recognises.
- **A short refresh,** not a lecture: three minutes on what changed this year, then practice.
- **A router to practice.** Each learner leaves with a private year profile and up to two spotlights for the year ahead, delivered as modules and monthly drills.
- **What it is not:** a re-run of every module, a test of trivia, or a way to rank staff.

### Why once a year isn't enough on its own

- **Skills fade.** In a field study of 409 employees at a German public administration, people's improved ability to tell phishing from genuine email held at four months after training but had gone by six; video and interactive reminders helped (Reinheimer and others, 2020).
- **Annual training alone shows little effect.** In an eight-month randomised study of more than 19,500 employees at a US healthcare organisation, there was no significant relationship between whether people had recently completed annual awareness training and their likelihood of failing a phishing simulation (Ho and others, 2025).
- **Reporting does work at scale.** In a 15-month study of more than 14,000 employees, staff reports detected new phishing campaigns quickly and reliably (Lain, Kostiainen and Čapkun, 2022).
- **So the course is built for spacing and practice:** monthly micro-drills between capstones (spaced practice: Cepeda and others, 2006), a check that is itself retrieval practice (Roediger and Karpicke, 2006), and a capstone that measures habits and points to the next practice. None of this replaces technical controls such as MFA and filtering; it is the human half of the defence.

### Chains: how one small moment leads to the next

A chain is a sequence in which each step uses what the last one gained. Seen one at a time, each step looks small: an MFA prompt approved to make it stop, a contact number shared, a supplier's "new bank details" passed on. Seen together, they are an attack.

- **Retool, 2023.** Text messages sent staff to a fake sign-in page; one person entered their details. A caller then used a deepfaked voice of a member of the IT team to get an MFA code, and the attacker took over the accounts of 27 cloud customers.
- **Marks and Spencer, 2025.** Impersonation involving a third party got the attackers in; months of disruption followed (core_00's concepts).
- **British Library, 2023.** Compromised credentials on a remote access server without MFA were the most likely way in, and the attackers were inside for at least three days before anyone knew (core_00's concepts).

**Where a chain breaks:**

| Link | What breaks it | Taught in |
|---|---|---|
| A sign-in prompt you didn't start | Deny it and report it | core_03 |
| A message that wants you to sign in, pay or share | Don't use the link; check another way; report | core_01, core_02 |
| A caller who wants a code, remote access or a change | Hang up; ring back on a number you already have | core_02 |
| A change of bank details | Call back on the number on record; pass it to Finance without vouching for it | core_11 |
| A person at the door | Ask who they're here to see; walk them to reception | core_10 |
| A file sent to the wrong place | Report it at once, so it can be recalled or deleted | core_04, core_05, core_06 |
| Any of the above, after the fact | Report it now: the sooner, the fewer links follow | core_04 |

The earliest report breaks the most links. In the game, every chain can be broken at any point by reporting, and the debrief shows the difference the timing made.

### Busy days and pressure

- **Attackers like busy, quiet or distracted times.** The FBI and the US Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency warned in 2021 that they had "observed an increase in highly impactful ransomware attacks occurring on holidays and weekends", when offices are closed and IT teams are thin (advisory AA21-243A). Fraudsters lean on the same moments: year-end deadlines, holiday cover, the afternoon before a long weekend (core_11).
- **Pressure is the tell.** "Before the cut-off", "before the weekend", "Gillian's agreed it": urgency plus money, codes, data or access is the pattern to pause on (core_01, core_11).
- **Procedures exist for busy days.** Call-backs, two people approving, visitors escorted: these feel slowest when time is shortest, which is exactly why they're there. Keeping to them under pressure, including from senior names, is a skill the capstone assesses (C17-LO3).

### This year's picture (Edition 2027)

Rewritten for each edition from that year's sources, and re-checked before release. The 2027 draft, from what was published by October 2026:

- **Phishing is still the most common attack, and more often the only one.** In the Cyber Security Breaches Survey 2025/2026, phishing remained the most prevalent type of breach or attack by far (38% of businesses, 25% of charities); among organisations that had a breach or attack, the share whose only one was phishing rose to 51% of businesses and 57% of charities.
- **Serious incidents are rising.** The NCSC's Annual Review 2025 recorded 204 nationally significant incidents in the year to August 2025, up from 89, with ransomware reported most by academia, finance, engineering, retail, health and manufacturing.
- **Suppliers and stolen logins are common ways in.** Verizon's 2025 Data Breach Investigations Report found third parties involved in 30% of breaches (double the year before), and credential abuse (22%) the leading way in.
- **Impersonation over the phone and by voice.** The 2025 retail attacks reportedly began with calls impersonating staff (core_02, role_03), and voice cloning means a familiar voice is no longer proof (core_15).

Ama's three headline changes for the 2027 huddle (spoken lines are generic; any figures are on screen only):

1. **"Scams switch channels."** A text, then a call, then an email. Check another way, every time.
2. **"A voice isn't proof."** A voice note or call from someone you know can be faked. Call back on the number you already have.
3. **"Your login is the prize."** A prompt you didn't start, a caller who wants a code, a colleague who wants your password: all the same request.
