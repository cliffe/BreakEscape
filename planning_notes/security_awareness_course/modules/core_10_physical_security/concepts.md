# core_10 concepts: Physical security

This file is the source of truth for the module. The game, the explainers, the knowledge check and the reference card must all match it. Phone and text pretexting is core_02; the detail of printing and disposal is core_06; devices outside the office are core_09.

## Key messages

1. **If you do not recognise someone, help them, don't ignore them.** A friendly "Can I help you find someone? I'll walk you to reception" stops a tailgater without a confrontation, and genuinely helps a lost visitor.
2. **Do not hold a secure door for someone you do not know,** and do not prop fire or secure doors. Everyone badges in for themselves.
3. **Look after your badge.** Wear it inside, keep it out of sight outside, never lend it, and report a lost badge at once. A badge left on a desk or a table can be copied.
4. **Clear your desk and lock your screen.** A locked screen and a tidy desk mean a passer-by, a visitor or a cleaner cannot read confidential information or sit down at your account.
5. **Report physical security concerns,** including your own slip. Letting someone in by mistake and saying so quickly is a good outcome, not a telling-off.

## Concepts

### Tailgating and challenging

Tailgating is following an authorised person through a secure door without badging in. It is usually not aggressive: someone carries boxes or coffees, or just walks close behind and smiles, and good manners do the rest. The defence is also social. You do not have to confront anyone. A warm, open line works best:

> "Hi, can I help you find someone? I'll walk you to reception."

If they belong, you have helped them. If they do not, you have stopped them without a scene, and you can report it. The one thing not to do is hold a secure door open for a stranger, or wave someone through because challenging feels awkward. If you are not comfortable challenging, that is fine: step back and report it to reception, security or facilities, who will.

### Visitors, contractors and people with a story

Visitors sign in, wear a visitor badge, and are escorted; they are not left to wander. A contractor, engineer, inspector or cleaner with a believable story and a hi-vis jacket is a common pretext, because a uniform and a clipboard make people helpful. The checks are simple: are they expected today, is their name on the visitor list, does their work order and ID match the job and the firm, and who is escorting them? A genuine engineer will not mind waiting at reception to be signed in and escorted. Anyone who pushes to skip that, or who wants to be left alone in a secure area, is the one to report. Verifying a visitor is covered here at awareness level; doing it as a job (reception, service desk) is role_06 and role_03.

### Badges and passes

A badge is a key. Treat it like one:

- **Wear it inside** so colleagues can see you belong, and so a stranger without one stands out.
- **Keep it out of sight outside** (on the train, in the street, in a photo): a visible badge shows your employer, your name and often your access level, which helps an attacker build a pretext (core_12).
- **Never lend it or let someone use yours.** Everyone badges in for themselves, so the record is right and so nobody tailgates on your card.
- **Report a lost or stolen badge at once** so it can be cancelled. A missing badge is a key on the loose.
- **A badge can be copied.** Many access cards can be read at short range, so a badge left on a desk, a canteen table or a lanyard on a chair can be cloned by someone who gets close. This is not a reason to panic; it is a reason not to leave your badge lying around, and to report one that goes missing.

### Secure areas

Some areas (the comms room, server room, finance, HR) are restricted because of what is in them. Only people with a need to be there go in, they badge in themselves, and they do not let others follow. A propped or taped-open secure door defeats the lock entirely. If you find a secure door propped, close it and report it.

### Clear desk and clear screen

- **Lock your screen** whenever you step away, even for a minute (a keyboard shortcut does it instantly). An unlocked screen is your email, your files and your identity, open to anyone who sits down.
- **Clear your desk** of confidential papers, notes and removable media when you leave it, and at the end of the day. Lock them away.
- **Whiteboards and flip charts** in meeting rooms often hold client names, systems and plans. Wipe them after a meeting.
- **Do not write passwords on notes** left on or under the keyboard, on the monitor, or in a desk drawer (core_03).

### Printing and post

Collect printing straight away, especially from shared printers, and use secure release (where you print only when you are at the machine) for confidential documents. Do not leave printouts in the tray. Confidential paper goes in the locked confidential waste, not the shredder or the recycling (see core_06). The detail of classification, sending and disposal is core_06; here the point is simply: do not leave confidential paper lying around, and dispose of it properly.

### Doors, codes and keys

- **Fire doors** are there to hold back fire and smoke. Wedging or propping a fire door open defeats it, and official fire safety guidance for offices and shops lists "fire doors being wedged open" as a way fire and smoke spread. If a door genuinely needs to stay open, it should be held by an automatic hold-open device linked to the fire alarm, which releases it when the alarm sounds, not by a wedge, a bin or a fire extinguisher.
- **Door codes** should not be shared, written on or near the door, or read out where someone can overhear or watch your hand (shoulder surfing). Change a code that may have been seen.
- **Keys** are signed out and back, and a lost key is reported.

### Unexpected deliveries and found items

An unexpected parcel, a USB stick left in the car park, or equipment that arrives without an order can all be a way in. Do not plug in a found USB stick; hand it to IT (core_13). Treat an unexpected delivery or a "left for you" device with the same care as a message from a stranger.

### Card payment devices (where staff take payments)

If your role involves a card payment terminal, check it at the start of a shift and during the day for signs of tampering or substitution: extra attachments, a device that looks different, loose or mismatched parts, or a reader that has been swapped. Report anything odd and do not use a device you are unsure about. (This is a PCI DSS requirement for staff at card terminals; the detail is role_02 and role_06.)

### Why reporting matters

The security and facilities teams can only act on what they are told. A quick report of a propped door, an unknown person, a lost badge or a suspicious device lets them check CCTV, cancel a badge, escort a visitor out or fix a door before anything happens. Reporting your own mistake (holding a door, losing a badge) quickly turns a possible incident into a non-event, and the person who reports is thanked, not blamed.

## What to do (the procedure)

As the reference card shows it:

1. **Challenge or report** anyone you do not recognise, politely: "Can I help you find someone? I'll walk you to reception." Do not hold a secure door for a stranger.
2. **Badge:** wear it inside, hide it outside, never lend it, report a loss at once.
3. **Clear and lock:** lock your screen when you step away, clear confidential papers and media, wipe the whiteboard.
4. **Doors:** never prop a fire or secure door; report one that is propped.
5. **Report** any concern, including your own slip, to security or facilities.

## Misconceptions and outdated advice

| Belief | The current position |
|---|---|
| "It's rude to challenge someone." | A friendly "can I help you find someone?" is not rude; it helps real visitors and stops the rest. If you would rather not, report it instead. |
| "They had a hi-vis jacket and a clipboard, so they were staff." | A uniform and a clipboard are easy to get. Check they are expected, signed in and escorted. |
| "Holding the door for someone is just good manners." | Not at a secure door for a stranger. Everyone badges in for themselves. |
| "My badge is just an ID card." | It is a key, and it can often be copied at short range. Wear it inside, hide it outside, never leave it lying around, report a loss. |
| "Locking my screen for two minutes is over the top." | Two minutes is long enough for someone to read your email or act as you. Lock it every time. |
| "Propping the fire door is fine, it's a hot day." | A wedged fire door lets fire and smoke spread. Use the proper alarm-linked hold-open device, or keep it shut. |
| "If I let someone in by mistake, I should keep quiet." | Report it at once. A fast report is a good outcome; the harm comes from delay. |

## Real cases

Use as background and inspiration only. Game content uses fictional names.

- **Classified papers at a bus stop, June 2021.** Classified Ministry of Defence documents about a Royal Navy ship's passage were found at a bus stop in Kent and handed to the BBC. (Reported 27 June 2021.) A reminder that handling and clear desk are part of physical security.
- **Authorised physical penetration testing.** Security firms are hired to test buildings by tailgating, talking past reception and trying doors; their reports repeatedly conclude that a single polite challenge would have ended the test. In September 2019, two Coalfire testers, Gary DeMercurio and Justin Wynn, were arrested during an authorised job at the Dallas County Courthouse in Adel, Iowa, after a dispute over the scope of their written authorisation; the charges were later dropped. It is why testers carry a signed permission letter. Use only as inspiration for Jo's "authorised test" framing.
- **Opportunistic building access.** On 30 March 2019, Yujing Zhang talked her way into Mar-a-Lago while carrying a USB thumb drive containing malware, showing how far a confident story and poor challenge can carry someone. In the game, the equivalent is a fictional "inspector" who wants the comms room.
- **Tailgating in the wild.** Industry surveys of physical social engineering consistently find tailgating among the easiest ways into an office. (Use generically; cite a current source before quoting a figure.)

## Glossary

- **Tailgating:** following someone through a secure door without badging in.
- **Pretexting:** using a believable story and role (engineer, inspector, cleaner) to get access.
- **Badge or pass:** an access card that unlocks doors; a key in card form.
- **Clone (a badge):** copying a card's data so a second card opens the same doors.
- **Clear desk:** leaving no confidential papers or media out when you step away or leave.
- **Clear screen:** locking your screen so nobody can read or use it while you are away.
- **Secure area:** a restricted room (comms, server, finance, HR) entered only on a need-to basis.
- **Hold-open device:** an alarm-linked magnet that holds a fire door open and releases it when the alarm sounds.
- **Shoulder surfing:** reading a code, password or screen over someone's shoulder.
