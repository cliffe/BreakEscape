# core_12 concepts: Social media and oversharing

This file is the source of truth for the module. The game, the explainers, the knowledge check and the reference card must all match it. Phishing emails are core_01; phone and text pretexting is core_02; someone being recruited to act against their employer is core_14.

## Key messages

1. **Attackers do their homework, and most of it is public.** What you and your colleagues post adds up to a map of your organisation: who does what, what systems you use, when people are away.
2. **Small details do the damage.** A badge in a selfie, a screen or whiteboard in the background, a system named in a job advert, a holiday countdown, a pet's name: each helps a scammer sound convincing or answer a security question.
3. **Think before you post about work,** and keep your private details private. You do not need to lock yourself away; you need to leave out the parts that help an attacker.
4. **Tighten your privacy settings** and keep security-question answers (pet, school, birthday, first car) out of public view.
5. **Treat strangers who ask about your work with care, and report fake profiles and recruiters.** A real recruiter does not need a map of your company's systems.

## Concepts

### What attackers learn from public posts

Open-source intelligence (OSINT) is just information that is already public, gathered and joined up. For a workplace, the useful pieces include:

- **Who works where, and for whom.** Profiles on professional networks name the employer, the role, the team and the manager. A job advert names the systems a company is hiring people to run.
- **Faces, names and badges.** Team photos, "new starter" posts and conference selfies put names to faces, and often show a lanyard or pass.
- **Screens and whiteboards.** Office photos catch monitors with email or dashboards open, and whiteboards with client names, project names and go-live dates.
- **Patterns of life.** Regular posts, check-ins and location tags show where someone is and when, including when they are on holiday and the office is short-staffed.
- **Security-question answers.** Pets' names, first schools, birthdays, favourite teams and first cars are common "secret" answers, and people post them happily.

### How it is used

- **Spear phishing (core_01):** an email that names your manager, your project and your system, and lands while you are expecting something like it, is far more convincing than a generic one.
- **Pretext calls (core_02):** a caller who knows the right names and jargon sounds like an insider. "Oskar asked me to sort the access before he's back from his leave" works because the leave and the names were public.
- **Impersonation:** a fake profile copying a real colleague or senior leader can connect to staff, ask for favours, or lend weight to a scam.
- **Recruitment scams and fake recruiters:** a "recruiter" with a tempting role asks for "an overview of your company's systems", an org chart, or who runs IT, as "background for the application". That is reconnaissance, not recruitment.
- **Account takeover:** security-question answers harvested from posts help an attacker reset a password or pass a phone check.

### Think before you post about work

You do not have to stop using social media. The habit is to pause and leave out what helps an attacker:

- No badges, passes or door codes in photos.
- No screens, whiteboards or documents in the background; check what is behind you before you post or go live on a video call.
- Do not name internal systems, security tools or suppliers, and be careful what a "we're hiring, must know X" post gives away.
- Think about holiday and "working from home alone this week" posts, which say the office or the house is empty or short-staffed.
- Do not post from inside a secure area or a client site.
- Remember that "friends of friends" and old public posts are visible too, and that deleting a post does not unsend it if someone has already seen or saved it.

### Privacy settings and security questions

- **Review who can see your posts, your photos you are tagged in, your friends or connections list, and your old posts.** Set new posts to people you actually know, and tidy up the back catalogue.
- **Turn off or limit location sharing** and the automatic location tags on photos.
- **Protect security-question answers.** Where you can, use a made-up answer kept in your password manager rather than a real pet or school (core_03), because the real ones are often public.
- **Lock down the accounts themselves** with a strong, unique password and MFA (core_03), because a taken-over social account is used to scam your contacts.
- Settings change and default to "open" after updates, so check them now and then, not just once.

### Fake profiles and recruiters

Not everyone online is who they claim. Warning signs of a fake profile include a very new account, a stock or AI-looking photo, a thin history, a job title that does not quite fit, mutual connections who do not actually know them, and an unusually quick move to private messages. A genuine recruiter or business contact will not need a breakdown of your company's systems, security tools or org chart. If a stranger steers the conversation towards your work, your access or your colleagues, slow down, share nothing, and report it. The UK's "Think Before You Link" campaign was set up for exactly this, after large numbers of people were approached by fake profiles on professional networking sites.

### Who speaks for the organisation

Most staff are not authorised to speak publicly for their employer. Know who is (usually a communications team), and pass media questions, comment requests and anything sensitive to them rather than answering yourself. Posting your own opinion is fine within the organisation's policy; posting as if you speak for it is not.

### If you have overshared

Nobody is in trouble for a post. If you realise you have posted something risky (a badge, a screen, a system name), take it down, tell your manager or the security team if it was work-related, and tighten your settings. If a fake profile has connected with you or your colleagues, report it to the platform and to the security team so others can be warned.

## What to do (the procedure)

As the reference card shows it:

1. **Before you post about work, pause.** Is there a badge, a screen, a whiteboard, a system name, or a "we're away" detail in it? Leave it out.
2. **Set your privacy.** Limit who sees your posts, tags, connections and old content; limit location sharing.
3. **Protect the answers.** Keep pet, school, birthday and similar out of public view; use made-up security answers kept in your manager.
4. **Strangers asking about work:** share nothing, and report fake profiles and recruiters.
5. **Overshared? Report it.** Take it down and tell the security team; nobody is in trouble.

## Misconceptions and outdated advice

| Belief | The current position |
|---|---|
| "My profile is harmless; I'm not important." | Attackers target everyone, and your posts help them reach your colleagues and your employer. |
| "One holiday post can't matter." | It is the pattern that matters: a holiday plus a manager's name plus a system name is a ready-made pretext. |
| "It's fine, only my friends can see it." | Check: defaults change, "friends of friends" is wide, and tagged photos and old posts may be public. |
| "Deleting the post fixes it." | Deleting helps going forward, but anyone who already saw or saved it still has it. |
| "A recruiter needs to know our systems for the role." | A genuine recruiter does not need your company's systems or org chart. That is reconnaissance. |
| "Security questions are safe because they're secret." | Pets, schools and birthdays are often posted publicly. Use made-up answers kept in your manager. |
| "I can say what I like about work online." | Follow the social media policy, and leave speaking for the organisation to those authorised to. |

## Real cases

Use as background and inspiration only. Game content uses fictional names.

- **Strava heatmap, 2017 to 2018.** A public fitness-app heatmap, published in November 2017, was found in January 2018 to trace the layouts and routes of military bases worldwide, including forward bases overseas, because individuals' location data added up to something sensitive. The company then simplified its privacy settings. (Reported by The Guardian and others, January 2018.)
- **"Think Before You Link".** A UK government and security-services campaign warning that fake profiles on professional networking sites are used to approach and recruit people. MI5 reported that over 10,000 disguised approaches had been made to UK nationals on professional networking sites. (NPSA campaign and app, 2022.)
- **A leaked login on a video call, 2020.** On 20 November 2020, a Dutch journalist joined a confidential EU defence ministers' video meeting after the Dutch defence minister tweeted a photo that showed the meeting ID and most of the PIN. A reminder that a screen or a sign-in in a photo is sensitive.
- **The "Robin Sage" experiment, 2010.** A security researcher created a fictitious profile of a cyber analyst, which several hundred real defence and security professionals connected to, sharing work details and contacts. (Presented at Black Hat 2010.) An illustration of how readily a fake profile is trusted.

## Glossary

- **OSINT (open-source intelligence):** information that is already public, gathered and joined up.
- **Oversharing:** posting more detail than is safe, especially about work and personal "secret" answers.
- **Digital footprint:** everything about you that is findable online.
- **Spear phishing:** a targeted phishing message using details about you (core_01).
- **Pretext:** a believable story and role built from what an attacker has learned (core_02).
- **Fake profile:** an account impersonating a person or inventing one, used to connect and gather information.
- **Security question:** a "secret" answer (pet, school, birthday) used to recover an account; often guessable from posts.
- **Location tag / geotag:** a place attached to a post or photo, automatically or by hand.
