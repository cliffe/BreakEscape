## Critical behaviours

To complete the module the learner must, at least once:

- **C12-CB1 Tighten the key privacy settings on a profile** (C12-B4 not at Not yet).
- **C12-CB2 Decline a stranger's request for information about the organisation's systems or people, and report it** (C12-B5 not at Not yet).
- **C12-CB3 Correctly hold or tweak a clearly risky post** (C12-B3 not at Not yet).

A learner who misses a critical behaviour is taken back to it: Ama replays the relevant "Post or Not?" items or the settings step with the explanation, then a fresh item of the same kind appears. The record keeps both attempts.

## Knowledge check bank

Five items per sitting, covering every learning outcome. Stems are short workplace situations.

### C12-Q1 (LO1, LO2, multiple select)

A colleague posts a public "first day!" photo. Which parts could help an attacker? Choose all that apply.

- **A. Their lanyard and badge are visible.** *Feedback: A badge shows the employer and often the access level, and can sometimes be copied.*
- **B. A monitor with an email open is in the background.** *Feedback: Screens can reveal names, systems and content.*
- **C. A whiteboard behind them reads "Ledger go-live Dec".** *Feedback: A system name and a date are a gift for a pretext.*
- D. They look happy. *Feedback: Looking happy is not a security risk.*

### C12-Q2 (LO1, single choice)

How does an attacker usually turn someone's public posts into an attack?

- A. By hacking the social network. *Feedback: No hacking is needed; the information is already public.*
- **B. By joining up public details into a convincing phishing email or pretext call.** *Feedback: Right. A manager's name, a system and a holiday make a scam believable.*
- C. By posting comments on the person's photos. *Feedback: That is not the usual route.*
- D. They cannot, if the person has lots of followers. *Feedback: More followers does not make posts safer.*

### C12-Q3 (LO3, single choice)

You want to keep using social media but reduce the risk. What is the most useful first step?

- **A. Review who can see your posts, tags, connections and old content, and limit location sharing.** *Feedback: Right. Tightening privacy settings cuts off most of what an attacker gathers.*
- B. Delete your account. *Feedback: You do not have to; tighten settings and think before posting about work.*
- C. Stop posting photos of your pets. *Feedback: Pets are fine; just keep them out of your security answers.*
- D. Set a stronger password only. *Feedback: A strong password and MFA help, but review your privacy settings too.*

### C12-Q4 (LO4, single choice)

A recruiter messages you about an exciting role and, "to tailor the application", asks for an overview of your company's systems and who runs IT. What do you do?

- A. Send it; it shows you are keen and it is only background. *Feedback: A genuine recruiter does not need your company's systems or org chart. This is reconnaissance.*
- **B. Decline politely, share nothing about systems or people, and report the profile.** *Feedback: Right.*
- C. Share a little to keep them interested. *Feedback: Even a little helps build a pretext. Share nothing.*
- D. Ask colleagues to send it instead. *Feedback: Do not pass the request on; decline and report it.*

### C12-Q5 (LO3, true or false with reason)

"Using my dog's real name as a security-question answer is fine, because only I know it."

- A. True: a pet's name is private. *Feedback: Pets' names are often posted publicly, so they are easy to find.*
- **B. False: use a made-up answer kept in your password manager, because real pet names are often public.** *Feedback: Right.*

### C12-Q6 (LO2, single choice)

You are about to post a team photo taken in the office. What should you check first?

- **A. Whether any badges, screens, whiteboards or documents are visible, and crop or retake if so.** *Feedback: Right. Check the background before you post.*
- B. Whether everyone is smiling. *Feedback: Not a security matter.*
- C. Whether you have enough followers to make it worthwhile. *Feedback: Not relevant to the risk.*
- D. Nothing; office photos are always fine. *Feedback: Office photos often catch screens and whiteboards.*

### C12-Q7 (LO5, single choice)

A news site asks you, as an employee, to comment on a story about your company. What should you do?

- A. Give them a quick quote; you know the facts. *Feedback: Most staff are not authorised to speak for the organisation.*
- **B. Pass it to the communications team and do not comment yourself.** *Feedback: Right. They speak for the organisation.*
- C. Post your own view online instead. *Feedback: Posting as if you speak for the organisation is not your call; refer it on.*
- D. Ignore it entirely and tell no one. *Feedback: Pass it to communications so they can respond.*

### C12-Q8 (LO4, single choice)

You get a connection request from a profile in your CEO's name, but the account is new and has few connections. What is the best response?

- A. Accept it; it is the CEO. *Feedback: A new account in a senior leader's name is a common impersonation.*
- **B. Don't accept it; report it as a possible fake profile.** *Feedback: Right. Fake senior profiles are used to lend weight to later scams.*
- C. Message them to ask if it is really them. *Feedback: Check through a known channel, and report the profile.*
- D. Accept it but do not message them. *Feedback: Do not accept a likely fake; report it.*

### C12-Q9 (LO1, single choice)

Why can a "we're away for two weeks" holiday post be a problem for work?

- **A. It can signal that a home or an office is empty or short-staffed, which helps a scammer time an approach.** *Feedback: Right. Patterns of who is away are useful to an attacker.*
- B. It is never a problem. *Feedback: Combined with other details, it helps build a pretext.*
- C. Only if you name the airline. *Feedback: The "away" signal itself is the issue.*
- D. Only managers need to worry about it. *Feedback: It applies to everyone.*

## Reflection prompt

- "If someone spent ten minutes looking at your public posts, what could they learn about where you work and how to reach your colleagues?"
- For facilitated sessions: "Where is the line, for us, between a normal life online and giving an attacker a head start, and how do we help each other stay the right side of it?"

## Completion and pass rules

- **Complete** when the learner has played through all beats (or the text route), shown each critical behaviour (with remediation if needed), passed the knowledge check, and opened the reference card.
- **Pass mark:** 4 of 5 (the organisation can change it; 80% default).
- **Retakes:** a failed check draws five new items. After two fails, Ama replays the Learn beat before the third attempt.
- **Test-out** (where allowed): the Apply beat and the check without the OSINT build and the drill. A pass records the module complete; missing a critical behaviour sends the learner through the full module.

## Evidence for auditors

The learner's record (HA-05) shows: module id and version, start and completion dates, time on task, route (game, text, test-out), each assessed behaviour's rating, critical behaviours shown or remediated, the check score, and whether the reference card was opened. This supports:

- NIST SP 800-53 Rev 5 AT-2(3) (social media exploitation);
- CIS Controls v8.1 14.2 (recognise social engineering) and 14.6 (recognise and report incidents);
- ISO/IEC 27001 Annex A 5.10 and 6.3, clause 7.3;
- NCSC CAF B6.

See `02_COMPLIANCE_MAPPING.md` for the full references (when present).

## Measuring effect

Outside the course, an organisation can watch:

- **Reports of fake profiles and suspicious approaches** to the security team, which should rise.
- **Oversharing incidents** (badges, screens, systems posted publicly), which should fall, tracked kindly and without naming individuals.
- **Privacy-settings uptake**, where an organisation runs an optional "tidy your footprint" session and counts participation (not content).
- **Spear-phishing success** against the organisation, which should fall as public detail shrinks (links to core_01 measures).
- **The hook-to-Apply change** (C12-B1 against C12-B3 and B4) across the organisation.

Any measurement of staff social media use must respect privacy: track behaviours and reports, not individuals' personal accounts, and keep anything optional genuinely optional.
