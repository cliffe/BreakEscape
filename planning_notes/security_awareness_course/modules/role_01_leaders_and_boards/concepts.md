# role_01 concepts: Leaders and boards

This file is the source of truth for the module. The game, the explainers, the knowledge check and the reference card must all match it. Facts were checked in October 2026; lines marked **check before each edition** change often.

## Key messages

1. **Cyber risk is business risk, and the board owns it.** Know what matters most to the organisation, what you would do if it stopped, and who decides.
2. **Prepare and practise before the crisis.** A plan with named roles, a way to talk when email is down, a decision log, a ransom stance, insurance you understand, and an exercise at least once a year.
3. **In a crisis, decide on advice, promptly, and write down why.** The clocks start when you become aware. Tell regulators, insurers, clients and the police on time with what you know, and update them as you learn more.
4. **Paying a ransom buys no guarantees.** It doesn't make recovery quick, it can't make stolen data safe, it doesn't meet or reduce your regulatory duties, and it may be unlawful. Decide your stance before you need it.
5. **Lead the people side.** Say what you know, what you don't and when you'll update. Look after your staff, don't hunt for someone to blame, and check any urgent instruction that claims to come from a leader.

## Concepts

### Why this is a board matter

An attack that stops core systems is a business crisis: lost income, missed duties to clients, regulatory action, harm to the people whose data is taken, and damage to trust. The technical team contains the attack and rebuilds. Only leaders can decide what the organisation will give up to contain it, what it will say, what it will spend and which risks it will accept. Those decisions go better when they have been thought through and rehearsed.

The UK picture, from the government's Cyber Security Breaches Survey 2025/2026 (published 30 April 2026; check the latest edition before quoting):

- 31% of businesses and 30% of charities had a board member with responsibility for cyber security (up from 27% of businesses the year before).
- 72% of businesses said cyber security was a high priority for senior management.
- 25% of businesses and 19% of charities had a formal incident response plan.
- 49% of businesses and 34% of charities had a rule or policy not to pay ransomware demands; 24% of businesses didn't know whether they had one.
- 16% of businesses and charities were aware of the Cyber Governance Code of Practice.

### The Cyber Governance Code of Practice (2025)

Published by the Department for Science, Innovation and Technology with the NCSC on 8 April 2025. It is voluntary and written for the boards and directors of medium and large organisations in the public and private sectors; smaller organisations are encouraged to follow its principles. The Code says what boards should do. The NCSC's Cyber Governance Training explains why and how, and the NCSC's Cyber Security Toolkit for Boards helps boards do it, including a document of questions for the board to ask.

The five principles, with the actions this module draws on (summarised; read the Code for the full wording):

| Principle | Actions this module uses |
|---|---|
| **A. Risk management** | A1 identify the technology, information and services critical to the organisation's objectives; A2 agree senior ownership of cyber risks and integrate them with enterprise risk management; A3 define and communicate the cyber risk appetite; A4 gain assurance that supplier risk is assessed in proportion to the risk; A5 regular risk assessments that reflect changes in the organisation, technology, regulation and threats |
| **B. Strategy** | B1 to B4: a cyber strategy aligned with the organisation's strategy and risk appetite, resourced and checked for delivery |
| **C. People** | C1 promote a culture of positive behaviours and accountability at all levels; C2 clear policies that support that culture; C3 **undertake training to improve your own cyber literacy**; C4 gain assurance, using suitable metrics, that the organisation's training, education and awareness programme is effective |
| **D. Incident planning, response and recovery** | D1 a plan to respond to and recover from an incident affecting critical technology, information and services; D2 **exercise the plan at least annually** with internal and external stakeholders, and feed the lessons back; D3 in an incident, take responsibility for regulatory obligations such as reporting, and support critical decisions and external communications; D4 a post-incident review whose lessons feed future risk assessments, plans and exercises |
| **E. Assurance and oversight** | E1 a cyber governance structure inside the wider governance; E2 formal reporting at least quarterly, with metrics and agreed tolerances; E3 regular two-way dialogue with senior executives, including the chief information security officer or equivalent; E4 integration with existing audit and assurance; E5 assurance that senior executives know the regulatory obligations and good practice |

The NCSC's **Cyber Governance Training** covers the same five principles: an introduction and five modules of about 20 minutes each, free, each with a short scenario video and five multiple-choice questions. This module is built to sit beside it. The NCSC training covers the board's role in general; Crisis Room practises the incident decisions, and can be run against the organisation's own plan.

### Duties in law and regulation

- **UK.** There is no general statutory duty for directors to take cyber training, and the Code is voluntary. Operators of essential services under the NIS Regulations 2018 must notify their competent authority of a NIS incident "without undue delay and in any event no later than 72 hours" after becoming aware (regulation 11). The **Cyber Security and Resilience (Network and Information Systems) Bill**, introduced on 12 November 2025, would bring more organisations into the NIS regime (for example medium and large managed service providers) and, as introduced, require an initial notification within 24 hours and a fuller report within 72 hours. In October 2026 it is still a Bill: Lords report stage is scheduled for 26 October 2026, and its text has no staff training duty. **Check before each edition.**
- **EU, NIS2 (Directive (EU) 2022/2555).** Article 20(1): the management bodies of essential and important entities must approve the measures the entity takes to manage cyber security risk (under Article 21), oversee their implementation, and can be held liable for infringements. Article 20(2): members of management bodies are required to follow training, so they can identify risks and assess risk-management practices and their impact on the entity's services; entities are encouraged to offer similar training to employees regularly. The detail is set by each Member State's law. Article 23 sets the reporting stages for significant incidents: an early warning within 24 hours, an incident notification within 72 hours and a final report within a month.
- **EU, DORA (Regulation (EU) 2022/2554), for financial entities.** Article 5(4): members of the management body must keep up to date with sufficient knowledge and skills to understand and assess ICT risk, including through regular specific training. Article 13(6): ICT security awareness programmes and digital operational resilience training for all employees and senior management.
- **Contracts and sector rules.** Client contracts (especially in the public sector) often carry their own notification clauses, and sector regulators have their own expectations. Know which apply before the crisis, and take legal advice on specifics.

### The plan and the crisis team

- **Named roles.** The plan says who leads at each level. Many organisations use Gold (leaders: priorities, money, communications, legal), Silver (the incident manager who coordinates the response) and Bronze (the people doing the work); Co-op described "a bronze, silver, gold approach" to MPs. The labels don't matter. Knowing who decides does.
- **Deputies.** The chief executive may be on a plane. The plan names who acts in their place, and that person acts.
- **Pre-authorised containment.** When an attack is spreading, waiting for a meeting to approve a shutdown costs hours. A good plan lets the technical lead isolate systems at once and tell leaders straight away; leaders then own the business impact.
- **A way to talk when email can't be trusted.** Assume email and chat may be down or read by the attackers. Agree an out-of-band channel in advance: a separate messaging group, backup phones, printed contact lists.
- **Help on call.** A retained incident response firm (Co-op "brought in our pre-agreed forensics teams, which we had on contract"), legal advice, the insurer's incident line and, for significant incidents, the NCSC.
- **Exercise it.** The Code asks for the plan to be exercised at least annually (D2). The NCSC's **Exercise in a Box** is free, offers tabletop (group discussion) and micro exercises, and lists ransomware among its topics. Co-op told MPs its leadership war-gaming "definitely paid dividends through the crisis".

### The first hours: decisions only leaders can take

- **Contain, at a cost.** Isolating networks or shutting systems down stops the spread but stops services too. As M&S put it to MPs, "there is a difference between choosing to take systems down and them being taken down". Decide on the technical lead's advice, accept the business impact, and record it.
- **Keep the lifeline services going.** Know which services matter most and how each runs without its systems. For Larkspur that is the cleaning rotas for hospitals and care homes, school deliveries and payroll. Paper fallbacks only work if they are tested: Co-op's were there, "but it is about how often you test them".
- **Bring in help and tell the insurer** as the policy requires.
- **Write it down.** The NCSC advises keeping a careful record of the response, including "decisions made, actions taken", offline or on systems the incident hasn't touched. The decision log protects the organisation with regulators and insurers, lets the next shift pick up, and is the backbone of the post-incident review. Each entry: time, decision, who decided, the advice taken, the reason, and when it will be reviewed.
- **Restore clean, not just fast.** Rebuilding takes weeks, not hours. The M&S chairman told MPs that people imagine "you change the fuse and turn the lights back on, but it does not work like that"; Co-op restored service about 14 days after the attack. Restoring from backups taken while attackers were inside can bring them back. Restore the lifeline services first, from backups known to be clean.

### Notification clocks

| Who | When | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| The Information Commission (the ICO), for a personal data breach | Without undue delay and, where feasible, within 72 hours of becoming aware, unless the breach is unlikely to result in a risk to people (UK GDPR Article 33) | Report what you know and add the rest in phases. The DPO advises; leaders own the decision. Since 30 September 2026 the Information Commissioner has been replaced by the Information Commission, which keeps the ICO name; the duty is unchanged. core_05 teaches the law |
| The people affected | Without undue delay, where the breach is likely to result in a high risk to them (Article 34) | Plain words, what was taken, what to watch for (for example scams using their details), and where to get help |
| NIS competent authority | Within 72 hours, for operators of essential services and relevant digital service providers | The Cyber Security and Resilience Bill would add a 24-hour initial notification (check before each edition) |
| EU regulators under NIS2 | Early warning within 24 hours, notification within 72 hours, final report within a month | For EU entities in scope |
| The insurer | As the policy says | Many policies ask for prompt notice and use of their own panel firms. Know the terms in advance |
| Clients | As the contracts say | Public sector contracts often have notification clauses |
| Police and the NCSC | As soon as possible | Report Fraud in England, Wales and Northern Ireland (it replaced Action Fraud in December 2025); Police Scotland on 101; the NCSC's incident reporting service for significant incidents |

The clock starts at awareness, not when the picture is complete. A first report that says "we don't yet know what was taken" is better than a late, tidy one.

### Ransom demands

- **What they look like now.** Criminals encrypt systems and copy data, then threaten to publish it on a leak site by a deadline, often posting a sample as proof. The data threat remains even when systems can be restored.
- **The NCSC's guidance** for organisations considering payment, written with the insurance bodies ABI, BIBA and IUA (14 May 2024), says "the ultimate decision whether to pay the ransom is with the victim" and asks victims to review it before paying. It lists what to consider: don't panic; review alternatives, including not paying; record your decision-making; consult experts where possible; involve the right people, including technical staff; assess the impact; investigate the root cause to avoid a repeat attack; be aware that payment does not guarantee access to your devices or data; consider the legal and regulatory position on payment; know that paying does not fulfil your regulatory obligations; report the incident to UK authorities. For publicly funded institutions the line is firmer: the British Library's review says the UK's national policy, articulated by the NCSC, "is unambiguously clear that no such payments should be made".
- **Legality.** Paying is not generally unlawful in the UK, but "payments may not be lawful, for example, if a ransom payment is made to an entity or area sanctioned by the UK" (NCSC, 2024), and the law of every country the organisation operates in applies. Take legal advice before any decision.
- **No guarantees.** "Even where a decryption key is acquired, it's unlikely to result in an immediate return to business as usual", and for stolen data "you cannot trust a promise to delete it once a ransom is paid" (NCSC, 2024).
- **Regulators.** The ICO does not treat paying criminals as reducing the risk to people, so paying will not reduce a penalty (joint NCSC and ICO letter to the Law Society and the Bar Council, July 2022; NCSC, 2024). Working with the NCSC may lead to "a more favourable regulatory response, including a lower fine from the ICO" (NCSC, 2024).
- **What three organisations did.** The British Library made no payment and did not engage with the attackers; its data was published. Co-op told MPs: "We did not pay a ransom, and nor did we contemplate or at any point discuss paying a ransom." M&S declined to discuss it publicly; its chairman framed the question as "what are you getting for it, once your systems are compromised and you are going to have to rebuild anyway?"
- **Decide in advance.** A stance agreed by the board (the rule, who may approve an exception, and which checks come first) means the first time the board debates paying is not at 11:00 on the worst day of the year.
- **UK policy (check before each edition).** The Home Office consulted from January to April 2025 and published its response on 22 July 2025. It proposed a targeted ban on ransom payments by public sector bodies (including the NHS, local councils and schools) and by owners and operators of critical national infrastructure; a payment prevention regime, under which other victims would report an intention to pay before paying; and mandatory incident reporting, with an initial report within 72 hours and a fuller one within 28 days. The government said it would keep developing all three, including how far the ban should reach into supply chains. As of October 2026 none of these is in force. Suppliers to public bodies, like Larkspur, should watch the supply chain question.

### Cyber insurance

- Insurance helps with the cost and the response (incident response, legal advice, public relations, business interruption). It "will not prevent a cyber breach/attack" (NCSC cyber insurance guidance).
- The NCSC suggests asking: what defences do we already have; how do we bring expertise together to assess a policy; do we understand the potential impacts of an incident; what does the policy cover and not cover; what security services are included and do we need them; does it include support during or after an incident; and what must be in place to claim or renew?
- Policies often set conditions (for example MFA on remote access, tested backups, prompt notification). Answer the insurer honestly, including about gaps you know of, and take advice. Hiding a gap can cost far more than the gap itself.
- Cover is a choice. M&S's general counsel told MPs that a year before the attack they realised they were "insuring for the trivia and not for the catastrophic" and changed the policy to cover the worst case. Co-op "chose not to specifically insure against this event", weighing impact, likelihood and the strength of its balance sheet. Both were deliberate board decisions.

### Suppliers

Many attacks arrive through a supplier's access. The British Library's most likely entry point was a server used for remote access by third parties without MFA; M&S said its point of entry "also involved a third party". Boards should know which suppliers can reach their systems, how, and whether every route has MFA (Code A4). Due diligence and contracts are role_07; service desk and remote tools are role_03.

### Communications

- **The formula:** what happened, in plain words; what we know; what we don't know yet; what we're doing; what you should do; when we'll update you next.
- **Staff first, or at the same time.** They will be asked, they may be worried about their own data, and they may be the first to hear rumours.
- **Clients:** meet the contract terms, give practical advice, and tell them how you will and won't contact them, because criminals send fake messages during well-known incidents.
- **The public and the press:** one spokesperson, no speculation, and no "no data was taken" until you know. Expect the attackers to contact the media: the M&S chairman told MPs of brushing his teeth while the BBC reported "a communication from the people who are allegedly attacking our business".
- **Use channels you trust.** If email may be compromised, say so and use another route.

### Leaders as targets

Leaders' names, voices and authority are the ones criminals most like to borrow: chief executive fraud, whaling, and cloned voices and faces (core_11 and core_15 teach these). A crisis makes it easier, because everyone expects unusual instructions. The defence is the same as for any member of staff: check another way, through the crisis group or a number you already have, and never act on an urgent instruction to pay or skip the process because of who it seems to come from. Leaders help most by saying, often and publicly: "I will never ask you to skip the checks."

### Who's behind it (optional explainer)

Most attacks on organisations like Larkspur come from criminals after money, using common weaknesses. Some come from an "advanced persistent threat": NIST describes "an adversary with sophisticated levels of expertise and significant resources" that pursues its objectives repeatedly over a long period, adapts to defenders' countermeasures and stays committed to its goals (NIST SP 800-30 Rev. 1, citing SP 800-39). Such groups often want quiet, lasting access or information rather than a quick payment. For a board the questions are the same ones, asked with a longer view: which of our information or access (for a supplier, its connections into clients' sites and systems) would interest a well-resourced attacker, and would we notice someone who stayed quiet for months? This short explainer covers NIST AT-2(5) at awareness level.

### People and culture

- **No blame.** Attacks are designed to exploit normal, helpful behaviour. Blaming the person who clicked, reset or approved stops the next person reporting (core_04). The Code asks boards to promote a culture of positive behaviours and accountability (C1).
- **Wellbeing.** The British Library's review says incident plans should include staff and user wellbeing, because attacks are "deeply upsetting". Long incidents need shifts, rest and thanks.
- **Leaders' own literacy.** The British Library's lessons include cyber-risk awareness at senior level, and recommend "the recruitment of a Board member or Board-level adviser with cyber expertise". The Code asks every director to train (C3).

### After the incident

- **Post-incident review** (Code D4): what happened, which decisions were made and why, what to change in the risk register, the plan and the next exercise. The British Library published its review so others could learn from it.
- **Add up the small risks.** The British Library's risk process escalated out-of-appetite risks well but was "less effective in modelling the amount of low-level risks being carried in aggregate". Ask to see the accepted risks together.
- **Expect another attempt.** "Once you have had one cyber-attack, you are more likely to have another" (M&S chairman to MPs).

## What to do (the procedure)

As the board's crisis card shows it.

**Before: ten questions to ask this quarter**

1. What are our lifeline services, and how does each one run on paper?
2. Who leads in an incident, who can isolate systems without waiting, and who stands in for the chief executive?
3. How will we talk if email is down or being read?
4. When did we last exercise the plan with leaders in the room, and what changed afterwards?
5. When did we last restore a critical service from backup, and how long did it take?
6. Which suppliers can reach our systems, and is MFA on every route in?
7. What does our insurance cover, what does it require of us, and what is the incident line?
8. What is our ransom stance, and where is it written down?
9. Which risks have we accepted, added together?
10. How do we hear about cyber risk, and how often? (At least quarterly, under Code action E2.)

**In the first hour**

1. Take the technical lead's advice on containment and back it. Accept the cost.
2. Convene the crisis team on the agreed out-of-band channel. Tell the chair and the chief executive.
3. Call the insurer's incident line and your retained responders.
4. Start the decision log: time, decision, who, advice, reason.

**In the first day**

5. Keep the lifeline services running on their fallbacks.
6. Start the clocks: the DPO on personal data (the ICO within 72 hours where feasible), any sector regulator, clients, the police (Report Fraud, or Police Scotland on 101) and the NCSC.
7. Tell staff, clients and the public what you know, what you don't, what you're doing, what they should do and when you'll update.
8. Put the response team on shifts.

**If there's a ransom demand**

9. Don't engage directly. Follow the agreed stance. Get legal and sanctions advice, tell the insurer and the NCSC, and record the reasons. Plan for the data being published whatever you decide.
10. Treat any urgent instruction to pay, even from a leader, as unverified until you have checked it on a number you already have.

**After**

11. Restore clean, lifeline services first. Hold a post-incident review and feed its lessons into the risk register, the plan and the next exercise.

## Misconceptions and outdated advice

| Belief | The current position |
|---|---|
| "Cyber security is an IT matter." | It is business risk. The Code asks boards to own it, and only leaders can make the trade-offs in an incident (Cyber Governance Code of Practice, 2025). |
| "We'll tell the ICO once we know the full picture." | The 72 hours run from awareness, where feasible. Report what you know and add detail in phases (UK GDPR Article 33). |
| "If we pay, we get our systems back quickly." | A key is not guaranteed, and even with one "it's unlikely to result in an immediate return to business as usual" (NCSC, 2024). You rebuild either way. |
| "If we pay, they'll delete what they took." | "You cannot trust a promise to delete it once a ransom is paid" (NCSC, 2024). |
| "Paying counts in our favour with the regulator." | The ICO doesn't treat payment as reducing the risk to people, and it won't reduce a penalty (NCSC and ICO, 2022; NCSC, 2024). |
| "Ransom payments are illegal in the UK." | Not in general, but a payment may be unlawful if it goes to a sanctioned person or area. Proposals to ban payments by public bodies and critical national infrastructure are not yet law (check before each edition). |
| "Our insurance will sort it out." | Insurance pays for help and some losses. It doesn't prevent an attack, and claims depend on the policy's conditions and honest answers (NCSC cyber insurance guidance). |
| "We have certification, so we're covered." | Certification is a baseline, not a plan. The British Library held Cyber Essentials Plus from 2019 to 2022; what mattered on the day was the plan, the architecture and recovery. |
| "Restoring from backup takes a few hours." | Rebuilds take weeks: "it will be months" (M&S chairman); Co-op restored service about 14 days after its attack. |
| "Say nothing until it's over." | Staff, clients and journalists will hear anyway, sometimes from the attackers. Say what you know and when you'll update. |
| "Every decision waits for the chief executive." | The plan names deputies and pre-authorises containment. An hour's delay can cost a day's spread. |
| "Nobody would impersonate a board member to our own staff." | Leaders' voices and names are the most borrowed, and a crisis is the ideal cover (core_15). |

## Real cases

Use these as background only. Game content uses fictional names.

- **British Library, 2023.** Rhysida ransomware on 28 October 2023; about 600GB of files stolen and later published when no ransom was paid; the likely entry point a third-party remote access server without MFA; legacy systems "the primary contributor" to the length of recovery; sixteen published lessons, including board-level cyber expertise, practising business continuity for a total outage, and staff wellbeing. (*Learning lessons from the cyber-attack: British Library cyber incident review*, 8 March 2024.)
- **Co-op, 2025.** Told MPs that attackers impersonated a colleague and answered security questions to get an account reset; that it restricted remote access and paused communication in the affected zone, preventing ransomware being deployed; that service was restored about 14 days later; that its leadership had war-gamed "this precise scenario"; that it did not pay or engage with the attackers; and that it had chosen not to insure specifically against this event. (House of Commons Business and Trade Sub-Committee, oral evidence HC 835, 8 July 2025.)
- **M&S, 2025.** Told MPs that initial entry on 17 April came through "sophisticated impersonation" involving a third party; that it chose to take systems down as a defence; that the loss of profit was estimated at £300 million gross, before insurance and other recoveries; that it would not discuss its dealings with the attackers; and that it supported mandatory reporting of serious attacks. (Same session, 8 July 2025.)
- **UK survey figures.** Board responsibility, incident plans and ransom policies, from the Cyber Security Breaches Survey 2025/2026 (DSIT, 30 April 2026).

## Glossary

- **Board:** the people who govern the organisation (directors of a company, trustees of a charity, governors of a school).
- **Non-executive director:** a board member who doesn't run the business day to day and challenges those who do.
- **Risk appetite:** how much risk the organisation is willing to accept to reach its goals.
- **Gold, Silver, Bronze:** one common naming of the three levels in a crisis: leaders, coordinators, doers.
- **Out-of-band:** a way to communicate that doesn't depend on the systems under attack.
- **Isolation:** cutting systems off from the network to stop an attack spreading.
- **Ransomware:** malicious software that locks files or systems, used with a demand for payment.
- **Double extortion:** encrypting systems and also threatening to publish stolen data.
- **Leak site:** a website where criminals publish or sell stolen data to pressure victims.
- **Decision log:** a written record of each decision in an incident, who made it, on what advice and why.
- **Breach coach:** a lawyer, often appointed through the insurer, who coordinates the legal side of a response.
- **Panel firm:** a responder or lawyer the insurer has approved.
- **Lifeline service:** a service the organisation must keep running, or restore first, because people depend on it.
- **Post-incident review:** a structured look back at what happened and what to change.
- **NIS, NIS2:** the UK's Network and Information Systems Regulations 2018 and the EU directive that replaced the EU's first NIS directive.
- **DORA:** the EU's Digital Operational Resilience Act for financial entities.
- **ICO:** the UK's data protection regulator, now the Information Commission.
