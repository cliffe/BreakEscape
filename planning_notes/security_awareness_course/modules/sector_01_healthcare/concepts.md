# sector_01 concepts: Healthcare staff

This file is the source of truth for the module. The game, the explainers, the knowledge check and the reference card must all match it. Data protection law in general is core_05, phishing core_01, reporting core_04, ransomware and updates core_07, and visitors core_10; this module covers how they apply on a ward.

## Key messages

1. **Only look at what you need for your work.** Every time you open a record it is logged. Looking out of curiosity, even at a famous patient or a relative, is a breach, a disciplinary matter, and can be a crime.
2. **Your smartcard is you.** Never share it or its passcode, take it out when you step away, and never work in someone else's session.
3. **Check who is asking before you say anything.** Don't confirm a patient is here unless they've agreed; take a name and number and pass it on.
4. **Share what care needs.** The duty to share information for care is as important as the duty to protect it. Check who you're sharing with, then use the approved route.
5. **When systems go down, use the downtime procedure, not a workaround.** Report anything odd, including your own mistakes, straight away.

## Concepts

### The rules behind confidentiality

- **The law.** Information patients share with health and care staff is confidential. Health data is special category data under UK GDPR, which needs extra protection (core_05). Under section 170 of the Data Protection Act 2018 it is an offence, knowingly or recklessly, "to obtain or disclose personal data without the consent of the controller", to retain it, or to sell it.
- **The Caldicott Principles.** Eight principles for using confidential information in health and social care, published by the National Data Guardian. The NDG revised them and added the eighth in December 2020:

| No. | Principle (exact title) |
|---|---|
| 1 | Justify the purpose(s) for using confidential information |
| 2 | Use confidential information only when it is necessary |
| 3 | Use the minimum necessary confidential information |
| 4 | Access to confidential information should be on a strict need-to-know basis |
| 5 | Everyone with access to confidential information should be aware of their responsibilities |
| 6 | Comply with the law |
| 7 | The duty to share information for individual care is as important as the duty to protect patient confidentiality |
| 8 | Inform patients and service users about how their confidential information is used |

- Principles 4 and 7 matter most on a ward. Principle 4: "Only those who need access to confidential information should have access to it, and then only to the items that they need to see." Principle 7: "Health and social care professionals should have the confidence to share confidential information in the best interests of patients and service users".
- **A Caldicott Guardian** is the senior person in a health or care organisation who advises on confidentiality and sharing. The principles advise involving one "where a novel and/or difficult judgment or decision is required".
- **Professional codes** say the same. The NMC Code, for example, asks nurses and midwives to "respect a person's right to privacy in all aspects of their care" (5.1) and to "share with people, their families and their carers, as far as the law allows, the information they want or need to know" (5.5).

### Only open what you need

- **Need to know.** Open a record because your work needs it, and open only the parts you need. A ward clerk updating the bed board needs a patient's name, date of birth, hospital number and bed, not their clinical history.
- **Every look is logged.** Record systems keep an audit trail of who opened which record, which parts and when. The logs are how inappropriate access comes to light: in the Worcestershire case below, a patient's complaint led to an investigation that found more than 1,800 views.
- **Well-known patients, colleagues and neighbours** are the records people are most tempted to open. They are no different: no need, no look.
- **Your own record and your family's.** Don't use work access to look at them. Use the routes any patient has: asking the care team, the NHS App, or a subject access request. The organisation's policy sets the detail.
- **The consequences.** Looking without a need is a personal data breach. It is a disciplinary matter, professional regulators can act, and under section 170 it can be a criminal offence, punishable by a fine (section 196). Curiosity is not a defence.

### Your smartcard is you

- **What it does.** An NHS smartcard (or another login token the organisation issues) proves who you are to national and local systems, and what you can see depends on your role.
- **The rules:**
  - never share your card or its passcode;
  - take it out whenever you step away, even for a minute;
  - never leave it in a shared computer;
  - never work in someone else's session, even to help, even in a hurry;
  - if you find a card left in, take it out, lock the screen, and give the card back to its owner or the nurse in charge;
  - report a lost card at once to the Registration Authority or the service desk.
- **Why.** Everything done under a card is recorded as its owner's work. Patient safety and the audit trail depend on knowing who did what. Using a colleague's session puts your actions in their name. (Acceptance of the national smartcard terms is mandatory before a card is issued, and those terms prohibit sharing; a breach is linked to disciplinary action. The current terms are the NHS Care Identity Service (CIS) terms and conditions, version 3.0, September 2025.)
- **Fix the system too.** If cards keep getting left in, the ward needs quicker ways to log in and out (more terminals, tap-in readers). Report it; don't just blame the nurse.

### Who's asking? Phone calls and visitors at the desk

- **You can't see a caller.** Caller ID can be faked, and knowing a patient's name and date of birth proves very little.
- **Patients decide who is told.** On admission, patients say who may be given updates: named people, or a password their family uses. The organisation decides how this is recorded.
- **Don't confirm** that someone is a patient unless they have agreed. Some callers are trying to find someone who doesn't want to be found; others are journalists.
- **Take a name and number,** and pass the message to the patient or the nurse in charge. Nobody is harmed by a short wait; someone can be harmed by a wrong answer.
- **Professionals** (a GP practice, a care home, another hospital) are called back on their organisation's number from a trusted directory before anything is shared.
- **The press:** never confirm anything. Pass every press call to the press office.
- **The police:** follow the organisation's procedure. The nurse in charge can act when someone's life is at risk; other requests go through the formal route to the information governance team.

### The duty to share

- **Not sharing can harm patients:** medicines missed at discharge, tests repeated, a care home that doesn't know what changed. Principle 7 exists because staff too often held back.
- **Share safely:** check who you are sharing with (a call back through the directory), share what is needed for the patient's care, and use the approved route (for example, secure email between registered NHS or care addresses, or the shared care record). Never a personal email address, never a messaging app the organisation hasn't approved.
- **Ask when unsure:** the nurse in charge, the information governance team or the Caldicott Guardian.
- **No surprises.** Principle 8 asks organisations to make sure patients know how their information is used, so sharing for their care should never come as a shock to them.

### Phishing in health and care

- **The lures look like ward work:** smartcard renewals, rotas, pension statements, payslips, test results, invoices from suppliers. Health and care staff are busy, often on shared computers, and used to urgent messages.
- **Shared mailboxes** (the ward's, the clinic's, the practice's) are a particular risk: a dozen people see the same email and each assumes someone else has reported it.
- **What to do:** report it through the organisation's route (NHSmail has its own reporting route; the organisation says which to use), tell the team not to click, and don't forward it around to ask. If you clicked or typed your password, report it straight away (core_01, core_04).

### When systems go down

- **Cyber attacks on health and care stop care,** not just computers. WannaCry in 2017 and the Synnovis attack in 2024 (below) cancelled appointments, diverted patients and slowed blood tests for months.
- **Downtime procedures** are the plan for that day: paper forms, printed patient lists from a downtime computer, results telephoned by the lab and read back with two identifiers, manual processes that the ward has practised. Know where your ward's downtime pack is.
- **Avoid unsafe workarounds.** Patient lists sent over personal messaging apps, photos of the whiteboard on a personal phone, personal email, USB sticks: each puts patient data somewhere nobody controls, at the moment the organisation can least afford a second incident. Use the downtime pack and the tools the organisation has approved (some approve a secure messaging app; it's an organisation slot).
- **Don't try to fix it yourself.** Follow IT's instructions, and report anything odd straight away: unexpected pop-ups, files that won't open, a message demanding payment (core_04, core_07).
- **Medical devices:** never plug a USB stick, a phone or a charger into a medical device or a clinical workstation. Devices can be disrupted or infected, and a device fault can put a patient at risk. Follow the organisation's medical devices policy.

### Photos, social media and talking about patients

- **Photos.** A picture of a patient, or of anything that identifies one (a whiteboard, a wristband, a screen in the background), is personal data and usually health data. Clinical photographs are taken only with consent, through the organisation's approved route, never on a personal phone.
- **Social media.** Nothing about patients, even without names. "Guess who's on our ward today" identifies someone.
- **Talking.** Corridors, lifts, the canteen and the bus are public. Curtains aren't walls: keep your voice down at the bedside (NMC Code 5.1).

### Visitors and staff areas

- **Staff-only areas** are for staff with ID. Wear yours. A polite challenge ("Can I help you? Who are you here to see?") is part of the job, and so is checking a story through the switchboard rather than the number a visitor offers.
- **Never leave a stranger alone** near computers, records or the medicines room. Call security if you're worried.
- **Contractors** (including Larkspur's engineers on client sites) sign in, wear ID, and expect to be checked. Visitors and building security in general are core_10.

### Reporting, and a just culture

- **Report:** phishing, lost smartcards, information sent to the wrong place, someone looking at records they shouldn't, strangers in staff areas, and your own mistakes.
- **Personal data breaches** go to the information governance team at once. The organisation then decides whether to notify the Information Commission (the ICO) within 72 hours (core_05). Since 30 September 2026 the regulator has been the Information Commission; it keeps the ICO name, and the duties are unchanged.
- **A just culture.** NHS England's guide for information governance and cyber security describes "a just culture" as "one that supports fairness, openness and learning", so that staff "feel confident to speak up rather than fearing blame", while holding people to account for gross negligence or deliberate acts.
- **Honest mistakes and deliberate acts are different.** Sending a letter to the wrong address is a mistake: report it and it will be handled fairly. Opening a record out of curiosity is a deliberate act. Telling the information governance team yourself is still the right thing to do, and the sooner the better.

### Why this training exists: the DSPT

- **The Data Security and Protection Toolkit (DSPT)** is the annual self-assessment for organisations with access to NHS patient data and systems in England. The 2026-27 toolkit (version 9) was released on 2 October 2026, is aligned to version 4.0 of the NCSC Cyber Assessment Framework, and is due by 30 June 2027.
- **NHS trusts, integrated care boards, commissioning support units and arm's length bodies** (and some independent providers) use the CAF-aligned toolkit. Training sits under outcomes B6.a (culture) and B6.b (training), which must meet the level NHS England sets. There is no percentage target: a training needs analysis decides what each staff group needs. Under CAF version 4.0, B6.b is "Not achieved" if training records are lacking or incomplete, or if success is measured only by how many people completed it; "Achieved" means each person's training is tracked and refreshed, and the activities are evaluated for reach and effectiveness.
- **For those organisations the old rule changed in 2023-24.** "At least 95% of staff trained using the national Data Security Awareness Level 1 e-learning or a local equivalent" was replaced by making sure all staff have "an appropriate understanding of information governance and cyber security".
- **Categories 3 and 4** (dentists, local authorities, opticians, pharmacies, social care, universities and others; and GP practices) still have the percentage rule: at least 95% of staff, directors, trustees and volunteers trained in data security and protection, and cyber security, in the last twelve months (DSPT 3.2.1), and an induction covering both (DSPT 2.1.1).
- This module, with the core modules, can form part of that training; the organisation's training needs analysis or DSPT category decides how it is used (`02_COMPLIANCE_MAPPING.md`, section 3.9).

## What to do (the procedure)

As the ward card shows it:

1. **Need it for your work?** Open it. Don't? Don't. Every look is logged.
2. **Card out, screen locked,** every time you step away. Never work in someone else's session.
3. **Who's asking?** Don't confirm someone is here unless they've agreed. Take a name and number and pass it on.
4. **Sharing for care?** Check who you're sharing with, then use the approved route. Share what's needed.
5. **Press, police or anyone unusual?** The nurse in charge, the press office or the information governance team.
6. **System down?** Use the downtime pack. No messaging apps, photos or USB sticks; nothing plugged into medical devices.
7. **Something odd, or a mistake of your own?** Report it now.

## Misconceptions and outdated advice

| Belief | The current position |
|---|---|
| "It's fine to look if I don't tell anyone." | Opening it without a need is the breach, and every look is logged. |
| "I can check my own record, or my mum's, at work." | Use the routes any patient has. Work access is for work. |
| "Confidentiality means not sharing." | The duty to share for individual care is as important as the duty to protect confidentiality (Caldicott Principle 7). |
| "If they know the patient's name and date of birth, they must be family." | Both are easy to find. Check what the patient has agreed. |
| "Using a colleague's open session is fine in an emergency." | Your actions go on their record. Log in as yourself and ask for help if you need it. |
| "A quick WhatsApp of the list is fine while the system's down." | Use the downtime procedure and approved tools. An outage is the worst time for a second incident. |
| "Charging a phone from the monitor's USB port is harmless." | Nothing gets plugged into medical devices. |
| "Someone will have reported the phish in the shared mailbox." | Everyone thinks that. Report it yourself. |
| "Data security training is box-ticking." | The DSPT and the CAF now judge whether training works and is tracked person by person, not only how many people finished it. |

## Real cases

Use these as background and inspiration only. Game content uses fictional names, and the game's outage is fictional too.

- **WannaCry, 2017.** The ransomware was released worldwide on Friday 12 May 2017. At least 81 of the 236 trusts in England were affected (a figure that includes trusts which switched systems off as a precaution), 37 of them infected and locked out of devices; 603 primary care and other NHS organisations were infected, including 595 GP practices. NHS England identified 6,912 cancelled appointments and estimated more than 19,000 in all; five A&E departments diverted patients. All the infected organisations had unpatched or unsupported Windows systems, and managing their internet-facing firewalls would also have protected them. No NHS organisation paid the ransom. (National Audit Office, *Investigation: WannaCry cyber attack and the NHS*, 27 October 2017.) The Department of Health and Social Care later estimated the cost at about £92 million (DHSC, October 2018).
- **Synnovis, 2024.** Synnovis, the pathology partnership between SYNLAB UK & Ireland and two NHS foundation trusts (Guy's and St Thomas', and King's College Hospital), suffered a ransomware attack on 3 June 2024 that hit almost all its IT systems and disrupted pathology services for hospitals and GP practices in south east London. Data taken from its working drives was published on 20 June 2024; services were fully restored by late autumn 2024, and notifications to affected organisations were completed by the end of November 2025. (Synnovis cyber incident updates.) Figures for postponed appointments and reported harm to patients are to verify before use.
- **Worcestershire Acute Hospitals NHS Trust, 2023.** A medical secretary in the ophthalmology department accessed the records of 156 patients more than 1,800 times without a business need; some were relatives and people living near her, and none had eye conditions. A patient's complaint led to the investigation. She pleaded guilty under section 170 of the Data Protection Act 2018 at Worcester Magistrates' Court on 15 November 2023 and was ordered to pay £648. The ICO's head of investigations said curiosity is no excuse. (ICO news, November 2023.)
- **The Caldicott Principles, 2020.** After a public consultation, the National Data Guardian published a revised set of eight principles on 8 December 2020, adding the eighth on informing patients ("no surprises").

## Glossary

- **Caldicott Principles:** eight principles for using confidential information in health and social care.
- **Caldicott Guardian:** the senior person who advises an organisation on confidentiality and information sharing.
- **Need to know:** having access only to the information your work requires.
- **Duty to share:** the duty to share the information care needs with the right people (Caldicott Principle 7).
- **Direct care:** care, treatment or support for an individual patient.
- **Audit trail:** the system's record of who opened which record, which parts, and when.
- **Smartcard:** the card (or other token) that logs you in to NHS systems and shows who did what; issued and governed under the NHS Care Identity Service (CIS).
- **Session:** the time a person is logged in on a computer; everything done in it is recorded as theirs.
- **Registration Authority:** the team that issues smartcards and manages their access.
- **Information governance (IG):** the rules and the team that look after how information is handled.
- **Downtime procedure:** the planned way to keep working when a system is unavailable.
- **Two-identifier read-back:** repeating back two details (such as name and date of birth) to confirm the right patient.
- **Shared mailbox:** an email inbox a whole team can see.
- **DSPT:** the Data Security and Protection Toolkit, the annual NHS self-assessment.
- **CAF:** the NCSC's Cyber Assessment Framework, which the DSPT for large NHS organisations follows.
- **Special category data:** personal data needing extra protection, including health data (core_05).
