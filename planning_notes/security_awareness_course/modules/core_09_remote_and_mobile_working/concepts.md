# core_09 concepts: Remote and mobile working

This file is the source of truth for the module. The game, the explainers, the knowledge check and the reference card must all match it.

## Key messages

1. **Out of sight, out of earshot.** In public, assume someone can see your screen and hear your calls. Save Confidential work and conversations for a private place, use a privacy screen, and lock your screen whenever you look away.
2. **With you, or locked away.** Devices and papers never sit unattended in a café, on a train, at a client's or in a car. Take only what the trip needs. Work devices are for you, not for family or friends.
3. **Lost it? Report it now.** Tell the service desk straight away, day or night, even if you think it will turn up and even if it's locked. They can lock or wipe it remotely, but only once they know.
4. **Check the network, then let the VPN do its job.** Ask the venue for its network's name, let the VPN connect, and never type your work password into a Wi-Fi page. Not sure? Use your phone's hotspot.
5. **At home, check what your camera and microphone pick up.** Look behind you before a video call, share a window rather than your whole screen, and keep work devices for work.

## Concepts

### Why working away from the office is different

In the office, a lot of security happens without anyone thinking about it: badge gates, colleagues who would notice a stranger at your desk, a managed network, confidential waste bins, a desk drawer that locks. On a train or in a café, the only control is you. Almost everything that goes wrong is ordinary rather than technical: someone reads a screen, someone hears a call, something is left behind. That's good news, because ordinary habits fix it.

The NCSC's guide for small organisations (April 2026) puts it plainly: phones and tablets "often leave the office environment and so are more likely to be lost or stolen". Its earlier small business guide (2020) gave the same reason for making sure every device can be tracked, locked or wiped remotely.

### Being overlooked

Shoulder surfing is reading someone's screen or papers over their shoulder, on purpose or not. Trains, planes, cafés, waiting rooms and client receptions are the usual places. People see more than you'd think: names in a spreadsheet, figures, the subject lines in an inbox, chat pop-ups, a photo of a whiteboard.

- **Choose your seat.** Sit with your back to a wall, a window or a bulkhead. An aisle seat with people behind you, or a table seat facing strangers, puts your screen on show.
- **Use a privacy screen** (a filter over the display that darkens it from the side). It helps against people beside you, not against someone directly behind.
- **Save Confidential and Restricted work for a private place.** On the train, do the Internal and Public parts of the job; open the residents list at the client's office.
- **Lock your screen whenever you look away:** Windows key and L on Windows, Control, Command and Q on a Mac, the power button on a phone. Automatic locking after a few minutes is a backstop, not the habit.
- **Turn off message previews** on the lock screen and pause pop-up notifications when working in public.
- **Papers count too.** Don't spread printouts on a table, and don't carry documents with the text facing out (the Bob Quick case, below).
- **People take photos in public.** A screen, a document or a lanyard can end up in the background of someone's picture.

### Being overheard

A phone call in a queue, on a platform or in a café is a broadcast to everyone nearby. Names, room numbers, door codes, addresses, account details and "the thing we can't tell the client yet" are all things people say on the phone without thinking.

- **"I'll call you back in ten minutes" is a complete answer.** Then call from somewhere private: outside, a meeting room, the car.
- **Never read out codes, passwords or account numbers in public.** Send them through the approved secure route instead (Larkspur's client portal), never by ordinary text message.
- **Headphones help you hear the other person; they don't stop others hearing you.**
- **Video calls in public** show your screen to the people behind you and play the other side's voice to the room unless you wear headphones.

### Devices and papers on the move

- **With you, or locked away.** Never leave a laptop, tablet or phone unattended in public, even for two minutes, even with a friendly stranger watching it. Take it with you to the counter or the toilet.
- **Cars:** avoid leaving devices in a car. If you have no choice, lock them in the boot before you set off rather than when you've parked, so nobody sees you do it.
- **Hotel rooms:** other people have keys. Keep devices with you, or locked away out of sight (in the room safe if there is one). If you'll be away from a laptop for a while, shut it down rather than leaving it asleep: device encryption protects a laptop best when it is switched off. (Organisation setting: check how your laptops are set up.)
- **Take only what you need.** Leave the full client archive at the office. View documents on the laptop rather than printing them, and don't carry a USB stick of everything "just in case".
- **Travelling abroad with a work device:** ask first (your manager, then the service desk). Some countries and some data need extra care, and the organisation may lend a clean device for the trip (organisation setting; core_16 covers asking first).
- **Collect everything when you leave:** the tablet on the café table, the charger, the papers under the coffee cup. Plenty of "lost" devices were simply left behind; a last look round the table stops it.
- **Charging:** use your own plug and cable, or a power bank. If your phone asks whether to trust a connected device or allow access to its data while charging, say no. (See the misconceptions table on public charging points.)

### Device basics

Away from the office, the device's own protections matter more. Most are set up by IT, and the job for staff is not to undo them.

- **Screen lock:** the NCSC recommends a 6-digit PIN or a strong password, and fingerprint or face recognition where possible (small organisations guide, 2026).
- **Encryption:** IT turns on device encryption for laptops and phones, so a lost device's data can't simply be read.
- **Updates:** install them when asked (core_07 covers updates in depth).
- **Apps:** only from official stores or the company portal.
- **Find my device and remote wipe:** managed through the organisation's device management, which is how the service desk can lock or wipe a lost device.
- **Personal devices for work:** only if the organisation allows it, and only through the approved app or work profile, with a screen lock. The organisation may be able to remove the work data remotely.

### Lost or stolen devices and papers

- **Report it straight away, day or night,** to the service desk's 24/7 line, even if you think you've only mislaid it and even if it's locked. If it turns up, they can switch it back on.
- **Say:** what it was, when and where you last had it, what was on it or signed in (email, chat, the survey app, the authenticator app), and whether it was locked.
- **What happens next:** the service desk locks the device, tries to locate it, signs it out of company services, and wipes it if it doesn't turn up. If it held your authenticator app, they help you move sign-in to another device.
- **Stolen?** Report the theft to the police as well; the service desk will say what they need.
- **Papers count.** Lost printouts with personal data on them may be a personal data breach; the DPO decides what happens next (core_05).
- **Nobody is in trouble for losing something.** The harm comes from the hours between losing it and saying so.

### Public Wi-Fi, in proportion

Advice on public Wi-Fi has changed, and older training overstates the risk.

- **What has changed:** most websites and work apps now encrypt what you send, so the old warning that "anyone on the café Wi-Fi can read your email" is mostly out of date. Larkspur's laptops also send their traffic through a VPN (a virtual private network: an encrypted tunnel back to the company) that connects by itself. The NCSC's device guidance recommends exactly this kind of automatic VPN that traffic can't bypass.
- **What is still a risk:** you can't easily tell who runs a hotspot, or prove it belongs to who you think it does (NCSC small business guide, 2020). Someone can set up a network with the same or a similar name as the venue's (an "evil twin") and show a fake login page that asks for your work email and password, or your card details.
- **What the venue's page should ask for:** accepting terms, sometimes an email address or a room number and surname. Never your work password.
- **The rules:**
  1. Check the network's name with staff or on the venue's sign. If two look alike, ask.
  2. On the venue's login page, accept the terms and nothing more. The NCSC notes that forced VPNs and these "captive portal" pages can clash; Larkspur's laptops show the page in a safe helper window first.
  3. Wait for the VPN to show it's connected before you open work.
  4. If a Wi-Fi page asks for your work password or card details, close it, disconnect, use your phone's hotspot, and report it.
  5. Not sure, or the VPN won't connect? Use your phone's hotspot: the NCSC's 2020 guide called using the mobile network instead of unknown hotspots "the simplest precaution".
  6. Turn off "auto-join" for public networks on your devices.
- **Stricter organisations:** some frameworks take a firmer line. The NHS Data Security and Protection Toolkit's guidance for smaller organisations asks them to tell staff that public Wi-Fi is unsafe. An organisation can set "hotspot only" in its profile, and the game and reference card follow it.

### Home working

- **Work devices are for work.** Not for children's homework, a partner's shopping or a friend's quick look. A borrowed laptop can have files opened, apps installed or work deleted, and games and free-download sites are a common way malware arrives. Offer another device instead.
- **Lock it when you step away,** even at home: housemates, visitors and children walk past.
- **Before a video call, check what's behind you.** Whiteboards, wall planners, printouts, sticky notes and screens in the background all show up on camera. The NCSC advises checking your surroundings before joining and considering background blur or an image (2026). Close the door, wear headphones for confidential calls, and mute when you're not speaking.
- **Share a window, not your whole screen,** so chat pop-ups, other documents and your inbox stay private.
- **Smart speakers and voice assistants** listen for a wake word and sometimes mishear it and start recording. Larkspur's default rule: mute them or move away from them during confidential calls.
- **Home Wi-Fi:** use the router's Wi-Fi password, keep the router up to date, change its default admin password if it still has one, and don't connect work devices to a neighbour's or an open network. (CIS safeguard 14.8 includes securing home networks.)
- **Printing at home:** avoid printing Confidential documents at home. If you must, keep the printouts safe and bring them back to the office for confidential waste; never the household recycling (core_06).
- **Not everyone has a quiet room.** Headphones, background blur, choosing when to take a confidential call, or coming into the office for it are all fine answers.

### What not to over-teach

- Working in public is normal and allowed. The module teaches how to do it safely, not to avoid it.
- Bystanders are almost always ordinary people. The passenger who reads your screen isn't a spy; the problem is that client information reached someone with no reason to see it.

## What to do (the procedure)

As the reference card shows it:

**Out of sight, out of earshot**

1. Sit where nobody is behind you. Privacy screen on.
2. Confidential work waits for a private place.
3. Looking away? Lock it: Windows key and L.
4. On a call in public: no names, codes or client details. "I'll call you back."

**With you, or locked away**

5. Never leave devices or papers unattended. Take only what you need, and look round before you leave.
6. Work devices are for you only.

**Check the network**

7. Ask the venue for the network's name. Accept the terms, then wait for the VPN.
8. A Wi-Fi page wants your work password? Close it and use your phone's hotspot.

**Lost or stolen?**

9. Call the service desk now, day or night: what it was, when and where you last had it, and what was on it.

**At home**

10. Before a video call, check what's behind you. Blur on, headphones in, door closed, share a window.

## Misconceptions and outdated advice

| Belief | The current position |
|---|---|
| "Never use public Wi-Fi." | Overstated for most organisations. With an automatic VPN and the venue's real network it is fine for most work; the risks are fake networks and fake login pages. Organisations may still choose "hotspot only" (the DSPT's line for smaller health and care organisations). |
| "On public Wi-Fi, anyone can read everything you do." | Mostly out of date: most web and app traffic is now encrypted, and the VPN covers the rest. What still catches people is typing a password into a fake Wi-Fi page. |
| "The VPN makes it all safe." | The VPN protects data on its way to the company. It doesn't stop you typing your password into a fake page, someone reading your screen, or the laptop being left behind. |
| "The café's Wi-Fi has a password, so it's private." | Everyone in the café has the same password. Treat it as public. |
| "It's locked, so I don't need to report it." | Report it anyway. The lock buys time; the service desk can wipe it, sign it out of company services and protect your accounts. |
| "I'll report it tomorrow if it hasn't turned up." | Report it now. If it turns up, they switch it back on; if it doesn't, the hours matter. |
| "Public charging points will steal your data." | A widely repeated warning. The FBI and FCC issued advisories in 2023, but neither cited a documented case, so the module doesn't dramatise it. The proportionate habit: your own plug and cable or a power bank, and say no if a phone asks to trust a device or share data while charging. |
| "Headphones mean nobody can hear my call." | They stop others hearing the other person. Everyone nearby still hears you. |
| "A privacy screen means I can work on anything." | It narrows the viewing angle from the side. Someone directly behind can still see, and it does nothing for calls. |
| "My hotel room is private." | Cleaners, maintenance staff and anyone with a key can come in. Keep devices with you or locked away. |
| "The kids know not to touch anything." | Children, partners and friends mean no harm, but a work device used by someone else can lose work, install apps or pick up malware. Offer another device. |
| "Background blur hides everything." | It helps, but check what the camera shows and what you share before you join. |

## Real cases

Use these as background and inspiration only. Game content uses fictional names.

- **Bob Quick, April 2009.** The Metropolitan Police's head of counter-terrorism arrived at 10 Downing Street carrying a document marked secret about a planned operation, and press photographers captured it. Police brought arrests forward and he resigned the next day. (BBC News, "Police chief quits over blunder", 9 April 2009; The Guardian, 9 April 2009.)
- **Ministry of Defence papers, June 2021.** Classified papers about HMS Defender's passage through waters off Crimea were found behind a bus stop in Kent and passed to the BBC. The senior official who lost them had his security clearance suspended. (BBC News, 27 June 2021.)
- **EU defence ministers' video conference, November 2020.** The Dutch defence minister posted a photo on social media that showed the meeting's login and part of its PIN, and a Dutch journalist joined the confidential call. (BBC News, "Dutch journalist gatecrashes EU defence video conference", 21 November 2020.)
- **NCSC on lost devices.** The NCSC's small business guide (2020) told organisations to make sure lost or stolen devices "can be tracked, locked or wiped", and its 2026 guide for small organisations repeats that phones and tablets are more likely to be lost or stolen because they leave the office.

## Glossary

- **Shoulder surfing:** reading someone's screen or papers over their shoulder.
- **Privacy screen (privacy filter):** a film over a screen that makes it hard to read from the side.
- **Public Wi-Fi:** a wireless network in a café, hotel, station or similar that anyone can join.
- **Hotspot:** a place offering Wi-Fi; also your phone sharing its mobile data connection with your laptop ("tethering").
- **Evil twin:** a fake Wi-Fi network named to look like a real one nearby.
- **Captive portal:** the page a public network shows before you can use it, usually to accept terms.
- **VPN (virtual private network):** an encrypted connection from your device back to the organisation.
- **Remote wipe:** erasing a lost device's data from a distance through device management.
- **Device management:** the system IT uses to set up, update, lock and wipe the organisation's devices.
- **Encryption:** scrambling data so only someone with the right key (your PIN or password) can read it.
- **Background blur:** a video call setting that hides what's behind you.
