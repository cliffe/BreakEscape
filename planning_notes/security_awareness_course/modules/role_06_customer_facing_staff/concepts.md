# role_06 concepts: Customer-facing and contact centre staff

This file is the source of truth for the module. The game, the explainers, the knowledge check and the reference card must all match it. Calls, texts and people at the door in general are taught in core_02; payment fraud aimed at the organisation is core_11; data protection law is core_05. This module covers what customer-facing staff do when the caller, the chat or the email is about a customer's account.

## Key messages

1. **Check every caller the same way, especially the ones in a hurry.** Urgency, anger and authority are reasons to keep to the procedure, not to skip it.
2. **Good checks use what the customer has, not what anyone can find.** Call back on the number already on the account, or let the system send an approval or reset to contact details already held, for the customer to complete themselves. Names, addresses, dates of birth and anything on a website are public or leaked.
3. **Watch for recent changes.** A new email address or phone number, followed by a reset, a new delivery address or a refund, is how accounts are taken over. Go back to the details from before the change.
4. **Protect the customer.** Share nothing with someone you haven't verified, never read details out, and if a customer is being scammed, tell them plainly and help them reach their bank.
5. **Card details only through the secure payment route.** Never write them down, type them, or take them by email or chat. And report every attempt, including the ones that failed.

## Concepts

### Why customer-facing staff are targeted

- **Advisers can change things:** contact details, logins, delivery addresses, refunds, payment methods, the list of people allowed to act on an account.
- **Advisers can see things:** names, addresses, order and payment history. That information makes a scam call to the customer, or a fake call to the organisation, sound real.
- **Calls are cheap.** A criminal can ring again and again until they reach a new, busy or kind adviser. Every adviser's procedure has to be the same, or the weakest link is whoever answers.
- **It's a crime to trick you.** Section 170 of the Data Protection Act 2018 makes it an offence to "procure the disclosure of personal data to another person without the consent of the controller", knowingly or recklessly. That is what "blagging" is. Advisers who follow the procedure are the defence, not the suspects.
- **Insiders can be targeted too.** Criminals sometimes approach contact centre staff with money for customer data (the Coinbase case below). Report any approach; insider recruitment is covered in core_14.

### What makes a good check

- **Things the caller knows** are weak when they are public or leaked: a name, an address, a date of birth, an account number printed on an invoice, the head teacher's name on a school website, a mother's maiden name.
- **Things the customer has** are stronger: a phone or email already on the account (a call back to it, or an approval or reset sent to it that the customer completes themselves), access to the customer portal (approve the request there), or a known person at the customer's organisation reached through its own number.
- **Caller ID is not a check.** The number shown can be faked ("spoofed").
- **Match the check to the request.** The organisation sets a ladder; Larkspur's default is:

| Request | Examples | Check |
|---|---|---|
| General information | Opening hours, product details, how to place an order | None |
| Account information | Order status, delivery date, a copy of an invoice | Standard: the caller gives the account number, the postcode on the account and their name, which must match a named contact. Documents go only to contact details already held |
| Changes and money | New email or phone, new delivery address, login reset, refund, new named contact, payment details | Step up: check the change history first, then call back on the number on the account (from before any recent change), or have the system send an approval or reset to contact details already held, which the customer completes themselves. Refunds go only to the original payment method |

- **Ask, don't tell.** Open questions ("What's the postcode on the account?") check the caller. Leading questions ("Is it still LS6 4QT?") hand them the answer.
- **Don't ask customers to read out codes.** Everyone is taught never to read a code to anyone (core_02, core_03), because scammers ring people pretending to be an organisation and ask for the code they've just triggered. If advisers asked for codes too, customers couldn't tell the difference. So Larkspur's step-ups are a call back, or an approval or reset that the system sends to details already held and the customer completes themselves; nothing secret is ever read to the adviser. Some organisations do verify callers with a read-back code. Where they do, the code goes only to details already held, only on a call the customer made, in a message that says what it's for and that "we will never call you to ask for it", and advisers never ask for a code on a call they made.
- **Genuine customers usually understand.** "To protect your account, I'll ring you back on the number we hold" is good service, not an accusation.

### Account takeover

- **The pattern:** first the contact details change (by a call, a web form or an email that looks right); then, a few days later, a reset, a new delivery address, an order, a refund or a new named contact. Alerts and codes now go to the criminal's new details, so the real customer doesn't notice until goods, money or data have gone.
- **Signs:** "I've got a new phone", "I'm locked out", "send it to my new email", several changes at once, urgency ("the order must go today"), a caller who knows a lot but can't do the step-up check, a recent change made through the web form.
- **What to do:** don't make the change or the reset on that call. Check the change history. Call back on the number from before the change, or on the customer's main number from an independent source (for a school, its own office line). If the real customer knows nothing, restore the old details, flag the account and report it.

### Pressure on the phone

- **The levers:** urgency ("the lorry leaves at noon"), authority ("this is the council's contracts manager"), anger and threats ("I'll have your job", "I'm recording this call"), sympathy ("I'm calling from hospital"), flattery, and insider jargon picked up from websites and old documents.
- **Anger is a tactic as well as a feeling.** Genuine customers get angry too, and the procedure treats both the same: politely, and without skipping a step. A useful line: *"I understand. I can't do that without checking, and here's how we can do it quickly."*
- **Escalate rather than give in.** Put the caller on hold and ask the team leader. Team leaders back advisers who follow the procedure, every time, including when the caller turns out to be genuine.
- **Abuse.** Follow the organisation's abusive caller policy. Advisers can end abusive calls.

### Personal data on calls, chats and emails

- **Only verified, authorised people, and only what's needed.** An account's named contacts can hear about the account; nobody else can, however plausible.
- **Don't confirm** that someone is a customer, or (for care services) that someone lives in a particular home, to an unverified caller.
- **Send documents only to contact details already held,** never to a "temporary" address given on the call or in a chat.
- **Chats and emails follow the same rules.** A display name or a signature proves nothing. Don't open unexpected attachments sent through chat (core_01).
- **Requests from third parties** (police, solicitors, journalists, debt collectors) for a customer's data go to the DPO (core_05).
- **Requests from customers for their own data** are subject access requests, whatever words they use: pass them to the DPO straight away (core_05).
- **On the floor:** speak quietly, lock your screen when you leave the desk, and keep notes in the system, not on paper.

### Customers who are being scammed

- **Advisers are sometimes the first to notice.** A customer may mention, almost in passing, a call or text "from you" that wasn't.
- **Signs:** someone claiming to be from your organisation (or the bank, or the police) asking for payment to a new account; urgency and threats ("by tonight, or she loses her room"); secrecy ("don't bother the care home about it"); being told to move money to a "safe account"; a courier coming to collect cash or bank cards; someone else on the line or in the room telling them what to say; a large, unusual payment.
- **What to do:**
  1. **Tell them plainly** what your organisation never does: *"We will never ask you to pay into a new account by phone."*
  2. **If money has gone, or might go,** urge them to ring their bank straight away. In the UK, **159** connects to banks that hold more than 99% of UK retail current accounts, and "cannot be spoofed or impersonated" (Stop Scams UK). The number on the back of their card works too.
  3. **Point them to Report Fraud** (England, Wales and Northern Ireland; it replaced Action Fraud in December 2025) or Police Scotland on 101. If someone is in danger, or a courier is on the way to collect money or cards, call 999.
  4. **Offer to let them check you:** *"Ring the number on your bill and ask for me."* Encouraging customers to verify the organisation is part of protecting them.
  5. **Report it inside the organisation.** The scammer knew details from somewhere, and other customers may be getting the same calls.
- **Be kind.** These are professional criminals, and anyone can be taken in. Say so. Never "you should have known".
- **Think about vulnerability.** Older, unwell, bereaved or stressed customers are targeted more often. With the customer's agreement, offer to involve someone they trust, and follow the organisation's vulnerable customer policy.

### Card payments by phone

- **Use the secure route.** Keypad entry with masking (the customer types the number on their phone keypad and the adviser hears only flat tones), a payment link sent to the customer, or an automated payment line. The adviser never hears or sees the full card number or the security code.
- **If a customer starts reading the number out,** stop them politely and switch to the secure route.
- **Never** write card numbers down, type them into notes, take them by email, chat, text or social media, or keep them "to put through later". The PCI Security Standards Council's guidance says card data must never be sent over an unencrypted messaging channel "such as chat, social media, SMS (short message service)/text, or e-mail". (Its guidance accepts paper only if it is stored securely and shredded; Larkspur's rule is simpler: never.)
- **The security code is never kept.** Storing sensitive authentication data, which includes the three or four digit security code, after the payment is authorised "is not permitted, even if the data is encrypted", and that includes call recordings (PCI SSC, 2018; PCI DSS v4.0.1 requirement 3.3.1, with the card security code covered specifically by 3.3.1.2). The secure route keeps it out of recordings in the first place.
- **If card details arrive by email or chat,** don't reply with them or copy them anywhere. Follow the organisation's procedure to delete them, and tell the customer how to pay safely.
- **Approaches.** Criminals sometimes approach or threaten advisers for the card details they hear. Report any approach at once (PCI SSC, 2018).

### Card terminals at a front desk

- Where staff take card payments in person, the terminal itself is a target. PCI DSS 9.5.1.3 expects staff to be trained to spot tampering and substitution, to check the identity of anyone claiming to be a repair engineer, and to report suspicious behaviour.
- **In practice:** know what your terminal looks like (seals, cables, the serial number on the inventory); don't let anyone repair, swap or take a terminal unless the visit is expected and confirmed through the payment provider's number already held (not the number on their paperwork); report anything odd. Visitors in general are core_10.

### Reporting

- **Report every suspected attempt, including the ones that failed.** One call is a nuisance; the same call to five accounts is a campaign, and the security team can only see it if each adviser reports their piece.
- **Include:** the time, the account, what the caller asked for, what they already knew, the number or address they used, and what you did.
- **Report through the organisation's route** (at Larkspur, the security team on extension 4444 or the fraud form on The Lark). Nobody is in trouble for reporting, or for a call that got past them and was reported quickly.

## What to do (the procedure)

As the reference card shows it:

1. **What are they asking for?** Information, account information, or a change or money?
2. **Check to match.** Ask, don't tell. Use what's already on the account.
3. **Changes and money: step up.** Check the change history, then call back on the number on the account (from before any recent change), or let the system send an approval or reset to details already held. Never use a number or address the caller gives you, and never ask a customer to read out a code.
4. **Under pressure?** Keep to the procedure. Put them on hold and ask your team leader.
5. **Customer being scammed?** Tell them plainly. Point them to their bank (159) and Report Fraud. Report it here.
6. **Card payments:** the secure route only. Never write, type, email or chat card details.
7. **Report every attempt,** even the ones that failed.

## Misconceptions and outdated advice

| Belief | The current position |
|---|---|
| "If they know the account number, postcode and date of birth, they're genuine." | All three can be found or bought. Changes and money need a step-up check using details already held. |
| "Caller ID shows the school's number, so it's the school." | Numbers can be faked. Call back on the number you hold. |
| "Send the reset to whichever number or email the caller gives." | Only to details already on the account. Otherwise it goes straight to whoever is calling. |
| "Read the details on file and let them confirm." | Leading questions hand the caller the answers. Ask open questions. |
| "Being strict is bad customer service." | Genuine customers want their accounts protected. Explain the check and make it quick. |
| "If they're this angry, they must be genuine." | Anger is a common tactic. Treat every caller the same way. |
| "Jot the card number down and put it through after the call." | Never. Use the secure route, so the number never reaches the adviser. |
| "Call recordings are secure, so card details on them are fine." | The security code must not be kept after authorisation, even encrypted. |
| "A failed attempt isn't worth reporting." | Failed attempts show the pattern, and the next call may reach a colleague who says yes. |
| "A scammed customer is the bank's problem." | You may be the first to notice. A minute of plain advice can save someone's savings. |
| "It's fine to ask a customer to read back a code; we sent it." | It teaches customers the very habit scammers use. Larkspur's advisers ring back, or let the customer approve the request themselves. Organisations that do use read-back codes keep them to calls the customer made, and never ask for one on a call they made. |

## Real cases

Use these as background and inspiration only. Game content uses fictional names.

- **Coinbase, 2025.** The company disclosed that a criminal group had paid "multiple contractors or employees working in support roles outside the United States" to collect customer data from internal systems (names, addresses, phone numbers, emails, masked identity numbers, ID images, account balances and history), then demanded money not to publish it. Coinbase refused to pay, estimated costs of US$180 million to US$400 million, and said it would reimburse eligible customers who had sent funds to the criminals as a result. (Coinbase Form 8-K, US Securities and Exchange Commission, 15 May 2025.)
- **PCI Security Standards Council, 2018.** Not a case but a warning from the standard-setter: staff who hear card details can be "approached and threatened by organized crime", and the safest design keeps card data out of the adviser's hearing and out of recordings. (*Information Supplement: Protecting Telephone-Based Payment Card Data*, v3.0, November 2018.)
- **The UK's 159 service.** Set up by banks, telecoms firms and technology companies through Stop Scams UK so customers can reach their bank safely after a suspicious call; it reports more than one million calls since it started (Stop Scams UK).

## Organisation-specific slots

| Slot | Default (Larkspur) |
|---|---|
| Ladder of checks | General information: none. Account information: account number, postcode and a named contact's name, asked not read out. Changes and money: change history checked, then a call back on the number held, or an approval or reset sent to details already held |
| Recent change window | Treat any change of contact details in the last 30 days as a reason to use the details from before it |
| Read-back codes | Off. If an organisation switches them on, the message reads: "Larkspur code: 482913. Give it only to a Larkspur adviser on a call YOU made to us. We will never call you to ask for it." (number generated per play, never voiced) |
| Refunds | Only to the original payment method |
| Secure payment route | Keypad entry with masking for calls; payment links for chats and emails |
| Card terminals | One terminal at the warehouse trade counter (turns on the front desk scene) |
| Promise to customers | "We will never ask you to pay into a new bank account by phone, text or email." |
| Fraud reporting route | The security team on extension 4444, or the fraud form on The Lark |
| Escalation | The team leader on the floor; the duty manager out of hours |
| Abusive callers | Warn once, then end the call and log it |
| Vulnerable customers | Note the customer's agreed support needs on the record; offer a trusted contact with their consent |

## Glossary

- **Verification:** checking that a caller is who they say they are, and is allowed to ask for what they want.
- **Step-up check:** a stronger check used for changes and money, such as a call back, or an approval sent to details already held.
- **Account takeover:** a criminal gaining control of a customer's account, usually by changing its contact details first.
- **Call back:** ending the call and ringing the customer on a number the organisation already holds.
- **One-time code:** a short code sent to a phone or email, which its owner types in themselves and never reads out to anyone.
- **Spoofing:** faking the phone number or email address a message appears to come from.
- **Blagging:** tricking someone into giving out personal data; an offence under the Data Protection Act 2018.
- **Named contact:** a person the customer has authorised to act on their account.
- **Vulnerable customer:** someone whose circumstances make them more likely to be harmed, for example through age, illness, bereavement or stress.
- **Safe account scam:** a criminal posing as a bank, the police or a company persuades someone to move their money to an account the criminal controls.
- **Secure payment route:** a way of taking card payments in which the adviser never hears or sees the full card details, such as masked keypad entry or a payment link.
- **Security code:** the three or four digits on a card used to check card-not-present payments; never stored after the payment.
- **Card terminal:** the device that reads cards at a counter.
- **159:** the UK phone number that connects you safely to your bank.
- **Report Fraud:** the national service for reporting fraud and cyber crime in England, Wales and Northern Ireland, which replaced Action Fraud in December 2025.
