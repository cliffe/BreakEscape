# core_16 concepts: Acceptable use and your responsibilities

This file is the source of truth for the module. The game show, the explainers, the knowledge check and the reference card must all match it. Where an answer depends on the organisation's own policy, this file gives Larkspur's default and the game uses the organisation's answer key (HA-11). Each topic's detail stays in its own module; this one is about responsibilities, reasons and acknowledgement.

## Key messages

1. **The small print has good reasons.** It protects the people whose data you hold, your colleagues and you. Every rule has a "because".
2. **Three answers: Allowed. Not allowed. Ask first.** No policy can list everything. When you're not sure, ask first: it's never the wrong answer.
3. **Five things are yours to do:** protect your access (never share passwords, codes, logins or badges); use approved tools, devices and storage; look at and share only the data your job needs; report anything suspicious, including your own mistakes; ask first when you're unsure.
4. **Work systems are monitored to keep them safe, within rules.** You must be told what, why and how. And write every work message as if the person it's about could read it, because one day they might.
5. **Read before you sign.** The summary, then the policy; ask about anything unclear; then acknowledge. If a rule gets in the way of your work, say so rather than working round it.

## Concepts

### What an acceptable use policy is

- **The organisation's rules for using its information, systems, devices and accounts,** and your own devices when you use them for work. It's usually one of a set: an overall information security policy, then topic policies (data protection, social media, personal devices, remote working), plus the disciplinary policy. Names differ by organisation (organisation slot).
- **Why standards ask for one, and for staff to know it:**
  - ISO 27001 has a control (A.5.10) requiring rules for the acceptable use of information and other assets to be identified, documented and implemented; ISO 27001 A.5.1 expects policies to be communicated to and acknowledged by relevant personnel; and cl. 7.3 requires everyone to be aware of the policy (secondary sources; the standard is paywalled).
  - PCI DSS 12.2.1 asks for acceptable use policies for end-user technologies that cover explicit approval by authorised people, the acceptable uses, and a list of approved hardware and software (secondary sources); PCI DSS 12.6.3.2 asks for training on them, and 12.6.3 for an acknowledgement at least every 12 months (`02_COMPLIANCE_MAPPING.md`).
  - NIST PL-4 (rules of behaviour) asks for a documented acknowledgement before someone is given access, and again when the rules change (secondary sources; FedRAMP sets "at least annually").
- **It applies to everyone** who uses the organisation's systems: staff at every level, temporary staff, contractors and volunteers.

### The three answers

- **Allowed:** you can, within the stated conditions ("at break times", "nothing confidential in shot", "through the company app").
- **Not allowed:** there's a good reason, and the show always gives it.
- **Ask first:** the answer depends on something the learner can't judge alone (the tool, the data, the client, the country), so someone who knows decides. It includes "allowed once approved", such as a software request.

"Ask first" matters most. New tools, apps and AI services appear every month, and no policy can name them all. An organisation where people ask is safer than one with a longer rulebook.

### Your five responsibilities

| Responsibility | Why | Detail in |
|---|---|---|
| **Protect your access:** never share passwords, MFA codes, logins or badges; lock your screen when you step away | Everything done with your account looks like you did it; a shared login is a door anyone can walk through | core_03, core_10 |
| **Use approved tools, devices and storage** | Unapproved apps and personal accounts put data where the organisation can't protect, back up or remove it | core_08, core_13, core_15 |
| **Look at and share only the data your job needs** | It's the law as well as the rule (see below) | core_05, core_06 |
| **Report anything suspicious, including your own mistakes** | Speed limits harm; no blame for honest slips | core_04 |
| **Ask first when unsure** | Guessing and workarounds cause most well-meant breaches | this module |

### Personal use

- Policies differ: many allow reasonable personal use of work devices and the internet; some don't. **This is an organisation slot**, and the game uses the organisation's own answer.
- **Larkspur default:** reasonable personal use is allowed in your own time (breaks, before and after work), as long as it doesn't affect your work or anyone else's, costs the organisation nothing, isn't illegal or offensive, and doesn't put systems at risk (no unapproved software, no personal files on shared drives).
- **The reasons behind the limits:** risky sites bring malware; heavy streaming slows shared connections (at Larkspur, the warehouse scanners share the Wi-Fi); the organisation's name is on everything that leaves its network; and personal browsing on a work device shows up in security logs (see monitoring).

### Monitoring and your privacy

- **What organisations commonly check, for security:** email and web filtering (scanning for malware and phishing), sign-in records, the health of devices, attempts to send certain data outside, access to sensitive systems, CCTV and door logs. What a particular organisation does is an organisation slot: its monitoring statement.
- **The law protects workers too.** Monitoring workers involves their personal data, so UK data protection law applies. The ICO's guidance on monitoring workers (October 2023; under review after the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025) expects employers to:
  - tell workers the nature, extent and reasons for any monitoring;
  - be clear about the purpose and collect no more than they need;
  - check that the monitoring is necessary and that no less intrusive way would do;
  - consider a data protection impact assessment, and complete one where the risk is high;
  - look at the content of emails and messages only in exceptional circumstances, telling workers in advance.
  Commentary on the guidance reads covert monitoring as justified only in exceptional cases, such as suspected crime (secondary sources; to verify against the guidance text before quoting).
- **Intercepting communications** on an organisation's own systems is governed by the Investigatory Powers Act 2016 and the business monitoring regulations made under it (SI 2018/356), which allow monitoring or recording of business-relevant communications on the business's own system, under conditions. Keep this to one line in the game; it's background for writers.
- **The limits are real.** In February 2024 the ICO ordered Serco Leisure and related trusts to stop using face and fingerprint scans to check staff attendance at 38 leisure centres: the employers hadn't shown it was necessary or proportionate, and staff weren't offered an alternative.
- **What this means for staff:** expect work systems to be monitored for security, and expect to be told how (The Lark's monitoring page). It isn't someone reading messages for fun. If something about monitoring worries you, ask the DPO.
- **Messages are records.** Anything written at work about a person can be part of what they receive if they make a subject access request (core_05), and messages can be disclosed in legal proceedings. Public bodies can be asked for information they hold under freedom of information law, which can include what suppliers like Larkspur send them. Tomasz's rule: *write every message as if the person it's about could read it.*

### Software, apps and AI tools

- Install and use only approved software, from the organisation's software portal or app store, or ask the service desk first. That includes free online tools (file converters, transcription sites, file transfer services), browser extensions and AI assistants.
- **Why:** unknown software can carry malware (core_07, core_08); a free online tool puts the file on another company's servers with no contract, no control and no way to get it back (core_13); unapproved AI tools may keep and learn from what's pasted in (core_15); and the organisation must know what it runs so it can update it.
- **Larkspur default:** "Ask first" for anything not in the software portal; the service desk answers most requests the same day.

### Your own devices

- **Organisation slot.** Some organisations allow work email and chat on personal phones, some issue work phones only.
- **Larkspur default:** work email and chat on your own phone only through the company apps, which keep work data in a separate work profile. IT can manage or wipe the work profile, not your photos or personal apps. No work files in personal apps or personal cloud storage. A lost phone with work apps on it is reported straight away (core_09).
- Background: the NCSC's guidance on bring your own device warns that full management of a personally owned device effectively turns it into a work device, which is why work profiles exist.

### Email, chat and calls

- Professional and respectful, as in any other part of work: the organisation's conduct policies apply to messages too (organisation slot).
- Messages are records (see "Monitoring and your privacy"). Don't forward work email to personal accounts (core_13). Check who you're sending to (core_06). Never send passwords or codes in a message or say them on a call (core_03).

### Social media

- Don't speak for the organisation unless that's your job; say views are your own when you mention work (organisation slot).
- Don't post confidential information, customer details, or photos that show screens, whiteboards or badges (core_12).
- Remember who's reading: posts about starting a new job, holidays and who you work for are what scammers build their messages from (core_00, core_12).

### Looking at data, and the law

- **Look only at the data your job needs.** UK GDPR Art 32(4) requires organisations to make sure anyone with access to personal data processes it only on their instructions; acceptable use policies are one of the ways they do it.
- **It can be a crime.** Under section 170 of the Data Protection Act 2018 it is an offence to knowingly or recklessly obtain or disclose personal data without the consent of the controller (the organisation), or to keep it afterwards; there are defences, for example where it was necessary to prevent or detect crime. Looking up a neighbour, an ex-partner, a famous customer or a friend's relative "just to check" is obtaining. In 2023 a former RAC employee was fined £5,000 under section 170 for taking accident victims' details.
- **Getting into systems you're not authorised to use** is an offence under section 1 of the Computer Misuse Act 1990 (up to two years in prison on indictment), and that includes using someone else's login to get round a control. Mention this calmly and once: the point is that "only what your job needs" is not just a house rule.

### Accounts, passwords and badges

- Your account is yours. Never share a password or an MFA code, never use a colleague's login even if they offer it, and never lend your badge (core_03, core_10).
- **Why it protects you:** the records show what was done with your account and your badge. If nobody else can use them, nobody can do something in your name.
- Lock your screen when you step away (the shortcut for the organisation's own computers is an organisation slot, shown on the reference card).

### Reporting

Reporting is a duty in almost every acceptable use policy, not a favour: suspicious messages, lost or stolen devices and badges, data sent to the wrong person, anything that looks like an incident, and your own mistakes, straight away, through the organisation's route (core_00, core_04).

### If the rules are broken: mistakes and misuse

- **Honest mistakes, reported quickly, are fixed and learnt from,** and the person who reports is thanked (core_00; NCSC CAF B6.a). A mistake you tell someone about is not misconduct.
- **Deliberate or reckless misuse is different:** looking up someone's data for personal reasons, sharing a login to dodge a control, copying data to take to a new job, using work systems for illegal or abusive content. It's handled through the organisation's disciplinary process (ISO 27001 includes a control for a formal disciplinary process) and sometimes the law (see above).
- **The difference is intent and openness.** ISO 27001 cl. 7.3 asks that everyone knows what follows if they don't follow the rules; this is how to say it fairly. Mei Lin's version: *"Mistakes, you report and we fix together. Doing it on purpose is different, and the policy says what happens."*

### Acknowledging a policy

- **What it means:** you've read it (at least the summary), you understand it, and you'll follow it. It records that the organisation told you. It doesn't take away your own rights, such as your data protection rights.
- **Why it's asked:** PCI DSS 12.6.3 requires an acknowledgement at least every 12 months; ISO 27001 cl. 7.3 requires awareness of the policy; NIST PL-4 asks for one before access and again when the rules change.
- **How it's recorded:** who, which policy, which version, when, and from where (in the game, the text route or the learner home), by the platform (HA-07, HA-05).
- **When you'll be asked again:** within 14 days of a significant change to the policy (one that changes what people must do), with a short "what changed" note, and once a year (organisation setting; Larkspur default 12 months). Minor changes such as a new phone number don't need a new acknowledgement (HA-07).
- **If something's unclear:** ask first, then acknowledge. "I have a question first" sends your question to the policy owner and keeps the acknowledgement open.

### When a rule gets in your way

A rule that stops people doing their job gets worked round, and a workaround is usually worse than the risk the rule was for. Say so instead: to the policy owner, the service desk or the security team. The NCSC's cyber security culture principles ask organisations to make security an enabler and to develop rules with input from across the business; telling them a rule doesn't work is part of that, and the course treats it as a good thing to do.

## What to do (the procedure)

As the reference card shows it, under **Small print, big reasons**:

1. **Can I?** Check the five-point summary, or search the policy on The Lark.
2. **Still not sure? Ask first:** the service desk for software and devices, the security team for anything suspicious, the DPO for personal data, your manager for how your team works.
3. **Made a mistake?** Report it now. Honest mistakes reported quickly are fixed, not punished.
4. **Rule in your way?** Tell the policy owner. Don't work round it.

Larkspur's five-point summary (the default for HA-11):

1. Work kit and accounts are for work. Reasonable personal use in your own time is fine.
2. Your passwords, codes, logins and badge are yours alone. Never share them.
3. Use approved software, devices and storage. Not on the list? Ask first.
4. Look at and share only the information your job needs, and handle it by its label.
5. Report anything suspicious, and your own mistakes, straight away. Work systems are monitored for security; The Lark says how.

## Misconceptions and outdated advice

| Belief | The current position |
|---|---|
| "Signing the policy is a formality. Nobody reads it." | The acknowledgement says you know the rules; an auditor may ask you what's in it. Read the summary and ask about anything unclear. |
| "If it isn't banned in writing, it's allowed." | No policy can list everything. If you're unsure, ask first. |
| "Any personal use of work kit is banned." | Many organisations allow reasonable personal use. Check yours. |
| "IT can read all my emails whenever they like." | Monitoring has to be necessary, proportionate and explained to staff; looking at the content of messages is for exceptional cases (ICO guidance). |
| "My work chat is private." | Work messages are records. They may be checked for security and disclosed, including to the person they're about. |
| "Using a colleague's login is fine if they said yes." | It isn't. It hides who did what, and getting round access controls can be an offence. Ask the service desk for proper access. |
| "Looking isn't the same as sharing." | Obtaining personal data without a work reason can be a crime on its own (Data Protection Act 2018, section 170). |
| "The rules protect the company, not me." | They protect you too: if your account and badge are yours alone, nobody can act in your name. |
| "Any mistake and I'll be disciplined." | Honest mistakes reported quickly are fixed together. Deliberate misuse is different. |
| "If a rule's daft, I'll just work round it." | Workarounds usually create the very risk the rule was for. Tell the policy owner; rules do change. |

## Real cases

Use these as background only. The game uses fictional names and organisations.

- **NHS Lanarkshire, 2023 (ICO reprimand).** Between April 2020 and April 2022, 26 staff had access to a WhatsApp group in which patient data was entered more than 500 times, and a non-staff member was added by mistake. WhatsApp had not been approved for patient data; the ICO found the board did not have the appropriate policies, guidance and processes in place when it was made available. The point for this module: an unclear rule plus a convenient tool led well-meaning people into a breach.
- **RAC, 2023 (prosecution).** A customer solutions specialist pleaded guilty to two offences under section 170 of the Data Protection Act 2018 after drivers complained of calls from claims management companies following accidents. He was the only employee to have accessed all 21 claims complained about, and was fined £5,000 with costs. The point: access logs show who looked.
- **Serco Leisure, 2024 (ICO enforcement notices).** The ICO ordered Serco Leisure, Serco Jersey and seven associated community leisure trusts to stop using face and fingerprint scans to monitor the attendance of more than 2,000 staff at 38 leisure centres, finding it was not shown to be necessary or proportionate and that staff weren't offered an alternative. The point: monitoring has limits that protect staff.

## Glossary

- **Acceptable use policy (AUP):** the organisation's rules for using its information, systems, devices and accounts.
- **Policy, procedure:** a policy says what the rules are and why; a procedure says how to do something step by step.
- **Acknowledgement:** your recorded confirmation that you've read and understood a policy and will follow it.
- **Reasonable personal use:** limited personal use of work kit, in your own time, within the conditions the policy sets.
- **Monitoring:** checking the use of work systems, for example for threats or misuse. Workers must be told about it.
- **Covert monitoring:** monitoring that workers aren't told about; only for exceptional cases.
- **Bring your own device (BYOD):** using your own phone or laptop for work, under the organisation's rules.
- **Work profile:** a separate, managed space on a phone for work apps and data.
- **Approved software:** software the organisation has checked and allows, usually from its software portal.
- **Need to know:** seeing only the information your job requires.
- **Just culture:** honest mistakes are learnt from; deliberate or reckless misuse is dealt with.
- **Disciplinary process:** the organisation's formal process for dealing with misconduct.
- **Subject access request:** a person's legal right to a copy of the personal data an organisation holds about them (core_05).
- **Policy owner:** the person responsible for a policy, who answers questions about it.
