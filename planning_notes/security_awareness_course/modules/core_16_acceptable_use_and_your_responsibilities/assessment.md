## Critical behaviours

To complete the module the learner must show, or be taken back through the practice for:

- **C16-CB1 Not installing unapproved software without asking** (C16-B7 not at Not yet).
- **C16-CB2 Not looking at personal data without a work reason** (C16-B8 not at Not yet).

They must also acknowledge each policy the organisation attached, after the summary has been shown ("I have a question first" holds the module open, with reminders, until they do), and pass the knowledge check (C16-B10: 4 of 5 by default).

A learner who misses C16-CB1 or C16-CB2 is taken back to it, following the course's remediation loop (`features/assessment_and_evidence.md`, section 4). Ama replays the matching cards with their reasons (S03, S14 and S20 for software; S16 and S19 for data), then a fresh situation arrives from the bank: a supplier's "free" spreadsheet add-in for software, or Leo asking whether his new neighbour is a customer for data. If it still isn't shown, a second loop uses a different situation and a more direct prompt. After two loops the learner goes on to the check: the record says "practised, not yet shown", and a private practice drill is scheduled two to four weeks later (HA-03's practice trigger, HA-21). Whether it is shown after a loop or not at all, the outcomes linked to that critical behaviour are capped at Developing for this sitting (`features/assessment_and_evidence.md`, section 3). Every attempt is kept in the record.

## Knowledge check bank

Five items per sitting, drawn so that LO2, LO3 and LO5 always appear and LO1, LO4 and LO6 rotate. **Org** items take their answer and feedback from the organisation's answer key (Larkspur defaults shown); **locked** items are the same everywhere. Stems are short workplace situations.

### C16-Q1 (LO2, locked, single choice)

A colleague who's off sick texts you: "Can you log in as me and send my report? The password's on the sticky note under my keyboard." What do you do?

- A. Log in and send it: they've given permission. *Feedback: Permission doesn't make it OK. It hides who did what, and getting round access controls can be an offence.*
- **B. Say no kindly, and ask the service desk to give you access or get the report another way.** *Feedback: Right. And suggest they move that password off the sticky note.*
- C. Log in, send it, then tell IT. *Feedback: Telling IT afterwards doesn't undo using someone else's account.*

### C16-Q2 (LO2, LO3, org, single choice)

You need to merge two PDFs for a report and find a free website that does it in seconds. What's the right move?

- A. Use it: it's only a PDF. *Feedback: The file goes to a company we have no contract with, and some free tools carry malware.*
- **B. Check the software portal or ask the service desk first.** *Feedback: Right. Larkspur has an approved tool for this. (Organisation answer: "Ask first".)*
- C. Use it, then delete the file from the site. *Feedback: You can't be sure it's deleted, or what was kept.*

### C16-Q3 (LO4, single choice)

Which statement about monitoring at work is true?

- A. The organisation can read any message, any time, for any reason. *Feedback: No. Monitoring must be necessary, proportionate and explained to staff.*
- **B. Work systems may be checked for security, and the organisation must tell you what it checks and why.** *Feedback: Right. Your organisation's statement is on The Lark.*
- C. Monitoring at work is illegal in the UK. *Feedback: Monitoring is allowed for legitimate reasons, within limits set by data protection law.*
- D. It only happens if you're suspected of something. *Feedback: Security checks such as email filtering cover everyone. Looking at message content is for exceptional cases.*

### C16-Q4 (LO3, single choice)

A council officer emails asking for a list of care home residents' names and phone numbers "for a survey". Who do you check with before sending anything?

- A. The service desk. *Feedback: They help with systems, not with decisions about sharing personal data.*
- **B. The DPO.** *Feedback: Right. Requests for personal data go to the DPO, who checks who's asking and why (core_05).*
- C. Nobody: it's a council. *Feedback: Anyone can claim to be from a council, and even genuine requests need a lawful reason.*
- D. Your manager. *Feedback: Worth telling them, but the decision on personal data sits with the DPO.*

### C16-Q5 (LO2, org, single choice)

There's a big match on at one o'clock. Can you stream it on your work laptop in your lunch break?

- **A. Allowed.** *Feedback: Larkspur allows reasonable personal use in your own time, as long as it doesn't slow things down for others. (Organisation answer and reason from the answer key.)*
- B. Not allowed. *Feedback: Many organisations do allow reasonable personal use. Check yours on The Lark.*
- C. Ask first. *Feedback: Asking is never wrong, and here the policy already answers it.*

### C16-Q6 (LO5, single choice)

One colleague clicked a dodgy link and reported it straight away. Another looked up a famous customer's address in a customer system out of curiosity. How are they handled?

- A. The same: both broke the rules. *Feedback: No. One is an honest mistake, reported quickly. The other is deliberate misuse.*
- **B. Differently: the first is thanked and helped; the second is a disciplinary matter and possibly a crime.** *Feedback: Right. The difference is intent and openness.*
- C. Neither is a problem if nothing bad happened. *Feedback: Looking without a work reason is the problem in itself.*

### C16-Q7 (LO2, LO5, locked, true or false with reason)

"If I only look at a customer's record and don't share it, I haven't done anything wrong."

- A. True: it only matters if you pass it on. *Feedback: Obtaining personal data without a work reason can be an offence on its own.*
- **B. False: look only at what your job needs. Looking without a work reason can be a crime (Data Protection Act 2018, section 170).** *Feedback: Right. And systems usually record who looked at what.*

### C16-Q8 (LO1, single choice)

Where do you find the acceptable use policy and its five-point summary?

- A. In the welcome email from your first day. *Feedback: It may have been attached, but the current version lives on The Lark.*
- **B. On the policy page on The Lark.** *Feedback: Right. It always shows the current version, and what changed.*
- C. Ask a colleague to forward you their copy. *Feedback: It may be out of date. Use The Lark.*

### C16-Q9 (LO6, ordering)

You're asked to acknowledge an updated policy. Put these in order.

1. **Read the summary and what changed.**
2. **Open the full policy for anything you're unsure about.**
3. **Ask about anything that's still unclear.**
4. **Acknowledge it.**

*Feedback: Read, check, ask, then sign. "I have a question first" keeps the acknowledgement open until you're happy.*

### C16-Q10 (LO2, LO3, org, single choice)

A client sends a meeting invitation for an app your organisation doesn't use, and the link wants you to install it. What do you do?

- A. Install it: the client's real and the meeting's real. *Feedback: The client may be genuine, but fake meeting apps are a common way in. Ask first.*
- **B. Ask the service desk, or suggest using your own organisation's meeting tool.** *Feedback: Right. Many can be joined in a browser with no install. (Organisation answer: "Ask first".)*
- C. Ignore the invitation. *Feedback: Safe, but the client is left waiting. Asking takes a minute.*

### C16-Q11 (LO4, LO5, true or false with reason)

"Work chat is private between me and the person I send it to, so it's fine to vent about a colleague there."

- A. True: nobody else sees it. *Feedback: Work messages are records. They can be checked, and disclosed, including to the person they're about.*
- **B. False: write every message as if the person it's about could read it.** *Feedback: Right. If something about a colleague is bothering you, talk to your manager.*

### C16-Q12 (LO3, LO5, single choice)

A rule stops you doing an important part of your job. What's the best move?

- A. Work round it quietly: the job matters more. *Feedback: Workarounds often create the very risk the rule was there for.*
- **B. Tell the policy owner or the service desk, and follow the rule while it's looked at.** *Feedback: Right. Rules do change when people say they don't work.*
- C. Ask a colleague how they get round it. *Feedback: Then two people are working round it, and nobody fixes the rule.*

## Reflection prompt

- "Which rule at work have you followed without knowing why? Who could you ask?"
- For facilitated sessions: "Which of our rules is hardest to follow in your team, and what would make the right way easier?" (Send the answers to the policy owner.)

## Completion and pass rules

- **Complete** when the learner has played through every beat (or the text route), shown each critical behaviour or been taken back through the practice for it, passed the check, acknowledged each policy the organisation attached, and opened the reference card.
- **Pass mark:** 4 of 5 (80%, the course default; the organisation can change it).
- **Retakes:** a failed check draws five new items. After two fails, Owain's card and the reasons round are replayed before the third attempt.
- **A pending question** keeps the module "in progress, awaiting acknowledgement", with reminders (HA-04). It is never recorded as a fail.
- **Test-out** (where the organisation allows it): the Apply beat, the check and the acknowledgement, without the show. Missing a critical behaviour sends the learner through the full module.
- **Re-acknowledgement:** when a policy changes, or a year after the last acknowledgement (organisation setting), the platform asks again (HA-07). The learner can do it from The Lark or the learner home without replaying the module; core_17 also offers it at the end of its day.

## Evidence for auditors

The learner's record (HA-05) shows: module id and version, start and completion dates, time on task, route, each assessed behaviour's rating, critical behaviours shown or remediated, the check score, and the version of the organisation's answer key the module used. Auditors see completion, the check and the acknowledgements per person; behaviour and outcome ratings in aggregate, unless the organisation chooses to include them (`features/assessment_and_evidence.md`, section 11). The policy record (HA-07) adds, for each policy: its name and version, the date and time of each acknowledgement, the route (game, text or learner home), any question asked and its answer, and the history of re-acknowledgements. Together they support:

- **PCI DSS 12.6.3.2** (training on acceptable use, in line with the organisation's own policies): met when the organisation's policy and answer key are loaded;
- **PCI DSS 12.6.3** (acknowledgement at least every 12 months) with **ISO 27001 A.5.1** (policies acknowledged by relevant personnel), and **PCI DSS 12.6.1**;
- **ISO 27001 cl. 7.3** and **ISO 27001 A.6.3**;
- **UK GDPR Art 24** and **UK GDPR Art 32(4)**;
- **NIS2 IR 10.1** and **DORA RTS Art 19**, for EU customers;
- **CMMC AT.L2-3.2.1** and **SOC 2 CC2.2**, for US customers.

See `02_COMPLIANCE_MAPPING.md` for the full references.

## Measuring effect

Outside the course, an organisation can watch:

- **Acknowledgement rate and time to acknowledge** after each policy change, by department.
- **Questions asked through "I have a question first",** and their themes. A cluster around one section means the rule is unclear or unworkable; that is useful, not a failure.
- **Software requests to the service desk** (which should rise at first) against **unapproved software found on devices** and unapproved cloud services seen in network logs (which should fall).
- **Access to personal data without a work task,** where the organisation's systems flag it, reported in aggregate only.
- **Two survey items** before and after (HA-14): "I know who to ask if I'm not sure something is allowed" and "I understand why our main rules exist".
- **Audit interviews:** whether staff can say what the policy covers and where it is (ISO 27001 cl. 7.3).

These need service desk and device management data, the organisation's own access logs and the survey (HA-14). Dashboards show them by department, never by named individual below the privacy threshold (HA-09).
