# core_05 concepts: Data protection and privacy

This file is the source of truth for the module. The game, the explainers, the knowledge check and the reference card must all match it. Legal detail is kept light on purpose: staff need to know what to do, not article numbers. The law was checked on 14 October 2026 against legislation.gov.uk, the regulator's guidance and GOV.UK; it changed in stages through 2025 and 2026, so check it again before each annual edition.

Boundaries: how to send, share, label, print and dispose of information safely (including redaction and hidden content) is taught in core_06, and this file points there. HR data in depth is role_05. General incident reporting is core_04; this module teaches the personal data part of it.

## Key messages

1. **Personal data is anything about a living person who can be identified,** even without a name. Some of it (health, ethnicity, religion, sexuality and a few more) is special category data and needs extra care. Behind every row in a spreadsheet is a person.
2. **Use it for the job, and share it only with people who need it.** Check who is asking. If you are not sure, offer to pass a message on, and ask the DPO.
3. **People have rights over their data, and they do not have to use the right words.** If someone asks to see, correct or delete their data, asks us to stop using it, or complains about how we used it, pass it to the DPO the same day.
4. **If personal data goes to the wrong place, is lost, or is seen by someone who should not see it, report it at once.** Do not wait to be sure, and do not try to fix it alone. The organisation may have only 72 hours to tell the regulator.
5. **Everyone makes mistakes. Reporting fast is what counts,** and nobody is in trouble for reporting.

## Concepts

### The law in one paragraph

In the UK, personal data is protected by the UK GDPR and the Data Protection Act 2018, both amended by the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025, whose changes have come into force in stages since June 2025. The regulator is the Information Commission (the ICO). It replaced the Information Commissioner on 30 September 2026 and kept the "ICO" name; its role and powers did not change. The ICO publishes guidance, handles complaints from the public, and can fine organisations up to £17.5 million or 4% of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher. The law puts its duties on organisations (a **controller** decides why and how personal data is used; a **processor** handles it on a controller's behalf), but it works through staff: every request, every breach and every disclosure passes through someone's hands first.

### What personal data is

Personal data is any information that relates to an identified or identifiable living person.

- **Directly:** a name, a staff number, a photo, a voice recording, an email address such as `firstname.lastname@`.
- **Indirectly:** details that together point to one person. "The van driver at the Wakefield depot who started in 2019 and works nights" may be only one person. A row with the name column deleted can still be personal data.
- **Opinions and notes count:** a complaint about a cleaner, a manager's note, a case note saying a customer "can be difficult". People can usually ask to see what is written about them, so write it as if they will read it.
- **Work details count:** a person's name with their work email address is personal data. Information about a company itself is not.
- **It doesn't matter where it is:** emails, spreadsheets, paper forms, CCTV, call recordings, photos on a phone, sticky notes, chat messages.
- **Not personal data under the UK GDPR:** information about people who have died, and statistics that are truly anonymous. Information about someone who has died can still be confidential, and it often includes details about living relatives, so treat it with the same care.
- **Pseudonymised data** (names swapped for codes, with the key kept somewhere else) is still personal data.

### Special category data and criminal offence data

- **Special category data** is personal data revealing racial or ethnic origin, political opinions, religious or philosophical beliefs, or trade union membership, and genetic data, biometric data used to identify someone, data about health, and data about a person's sex life or sexual orientation. The law sets tighter conditions for using it, and the harm if it leaks is greater. For staff: handle it with extra care and share it even more narrowly.
- **Where it turns up at work:** sickness absence reasons, occupational health notes, care home residents' care and continence notes, access needs, dietary notes given for religious reasons, union subscriptions on payslips.
- **Criminal offence data** covers convictions and offences, including allegations, proceedings and sentences (for example, criminal record check results). Treat it like special category data.
- **Sensitive but not special category:** bank details, National Insurance numbers, home addresses, dates of birth and children's details. Protect them carefully (fraudsters want them), but they are not on the special category list.

### The principles as five everyday habits

The law has seven principles. At a desk they become five habits, plus being able to show we follow them.

| Principle (as the law names it) | The habit at your desk |
|---|---|
| Lawfulness, fairness and transparency; purpose limitation | **Use it for the reason we have it,** in ways people would expect. A new use (marketing to people who made a claim, say) is checked with the DPO first. |
| Data minimisation | **Use only what's needed.** Collect, use and send only what the job needs; take out what the recipient doesn't need. |
| Accuracy | **Keep it right.** Fix mistakes when you find them. |
| Storage limitation | **Keep it only as long as needed,** as the retention schedule says, then dispose of it securely (core_06). |
| Integrity and confidentiality | **Keep it safe:** the right recipients, the right access, locked away when not in use. |
| Accountability | **Be able to show it:** records, reports and training (this module is part of the evidence). |

The Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 adjusted the rules on when data can be reused for a new purpose. For staff nothing changes: check a new use with the DPO before you start.

### Need to know: sharing with care

- **Share with people who need it for their job, and only what they need.** That covers colleagues as well as outsiders.
- **Data protection is not a reason to stop helping.** A driver who needs a resident's room number to return a cardigan should get it. Concerns about someone's safety (safeguarding) must still be shared through the organisation's safeguarding route; the law allows that.
- **Check who's asking.** On the phone you can't see who it is. Don't confirm whether someone works here or is a customer unless you know the caller should know. Offer to pass a message on, or to call back on a number already on file.
- **Requests from the police, councils, solicitors, journalists or anyone official:** take their name, organisation and contact details and pass the request to the DPO. The law does allow organisations to share personal data to prevent or detect crime or to safeguard someone, and since February 2026 the UK GDPR names crime and safeguarding as "recognised legitimate interests". But the decision is the DPO's, usually on a written request whose source has been checked. People pretend to be police to get addresses.
- **Only look up what you need.** Looking at someone's record out of curiosity is misuse of personal data. Knowingly or recklessly obtaining or disclosing personal data without the organisation's permission can be a criminal offence (Data Protection Act 2018, section 170). Honest mistakes are not offences: report them.

### People's rights, and recognising a request

People have rights over their personal data: to be told how it is used; to get a copy (a **subject access request**, or SAR); to have mistakes corrected; to have it deleted in some cases; to restrict or object to its use, including an absolute right to stop direct marketing; to move it to another provider in some cases; and rights about decisions made only by computer.

- **A request can take any form.** It can be spoken or written, including on social media; it can go to anyone in the organisation; it doesn't need to use the words "subject access" or mention the law. "Send me everything you've got on me", "what have you written about my mum?", "delete my number", "stop emailing me" and a solicitor's letter can all be requests.
- **Someone can ask on another person's behalf,** such as a relative or a solicitor, but they must show they have the authority (usually the person's signed authority, or a power of attorney). The DPO checks this; staff don't send anything.
- **The clock.** The organisation usually has one month to answer, counted from when the request arrives, or from when the person's identity is confirmed if that comes later. It can extend by two further months for complex or numerous requests, and the clock pauses while it asks the person to clarify what they want. It must make reasonable and proportionate searches. (These rules were written into the UK GDPR by the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025; the time-limit rules apply to requests received from 5 February 2026.)
- **What staff do:** pass it to the DPO the same day, through the organisation's route. Don't ask why they want it (their reason doesn't affect whether the request is valid). Don't gather or send the data yourself. Simple corrections that are part of normal work, like updating a phone number in the claims system, can be done as normal.
- **Complaints.** Since 19 June 2026, organisations must make it easy to complain about how they use personal data, acknowledge a complaint within 30 days and respond without undue delay (Data Protection Act 2018, section 164A). "I'm not happy you gave my number to the care home" goes to the DPO the same day, like a request.

### Personal data breaches

The law defines a personal data breach as a breach of security leading to the accidental or unlawful destruction, loss, alteration, unauthorised disclosure of, or access to, personal data. In plain words: **personal data goes somewhere it shouldn't, is seen by someone who shouldn't see it, or is lost, changed, destroyed or locked away.**

- **The ICO's examples:** sending personal data to the wrong recipient; devices with personal data lost or stolen; personal data changed without permission; personal data becoming unavailable.
- **At Larkspur that looks like:** an email to the wrong Sam; a laundry route sheet with residents' names left on a bus; a spreadsheet that went out with an extra worksheet; a shared folder open to everyone; ransomware locking the claims system for a day; a colleague looking up a neighbour's records; a caller talked into giving out an address.
- **Most breaches are not hacks.** The ICO's guidance says human error is the leading cause of reported data breaches. Being careful helps (core_06 teaches the checks); reporting fast is what limits the harm.
- **What the organisation must do,** and the DPO decides: record every breach, whether or not it is reported; tell the ICO without undue delay and, where feasible, within 72 hours of becoming aware of it, unless it is unlikely to result in a risk to people; tell the people affected without undue delay if it is likely to put them at high risk. When Larkspur handles data for a client (as a processor), it must tell that client without undue delay, and the client decides about the ICO.
- **Why speed matters.** The 72 hours include nights and weekends. In that time the DPO has to find out what happened, contain it, judge the risk and decide who to tell, and the sooner they start, the more of the harm they can stop (a deleted email, a closed link, a warned customer). Every hour you wait to report is an hour the DPO doesn't have, so don't leave a Friday slip until Monday.
- **Don't wait to be sure.** Staff don't decide whether something is a breach or whether it is serious. If you think it might be, report it.
- **Near misses count too.** The wrong file nearly sent, a folder found open to everyone that nobody seems to have looked at: report them. The ICO's guidance asks organisations to build a culture where staff feel able to report near misses.
- **Someone else's mistake in your inbox.** If you receive personal data you shouldn't have (another organisation's list, a misdirected email), don't open it further, don't forward it and don't use it. Tell the sender, report it to our DPO (we now hold data we shouldn't), and delete it when the DPO says so.

### What happens after you report

The DPO (with the security team where systems are involved) contains the breach: asking the recipient to delete the data and confirm, removing access, recalling or replacing documents. They assess the risk, decide who must be told, record it, and fix the cause so it doesn't happen again. You may be asked to help, for example by writing to the person you sent an email to by mistake: do that as well as reporting, never instead of it. Asking someone to delete a wrong email is a good step, but recalling a message may not work once it has left the organisation, so don't rely on it. Most misdirected emails end with a polite deletion. The DPO still needs to know, because some don't.

### No blame

Mistakes with personal data happen to careful people, usually when they are busy. What turns a mistake into harm is silence. Nobody at Larkspur is in trouble for reporting a breach, including their own, and people who report are thanked. Fines are for organisations that fail to protect data, not for staff who make an honest slip and say so. Deliberate misuse is different (see section 170 above).

### Other countries and personal life

- **EU customers:** the EU GDPR has the same core rules (the same article numbers, one month for requests, 72 hours for breaches); each country has its own regulator. The 2025 UK changes (the complaints duty, the reworded time limits, the Information Commission) don't apply.
- **US customers:** there is no single federal privacy law; state and sector laws apply. The module's habits still hold, and the organisation's own policy fills the slots (a localisation for later).
- **In personal life:** anyone in the UK can ask an organisation for a copy of their own data, and can complain to the organisation and then to the ICO (ico.org.uk).

## What to do (the procedure)

As the reference card shows it:

1. **Is it personal data?** It is if it is about a living person who could be identified, even without a name. Health, ethnicity, religion, sexuality, union membership, genetics and biometrics are special category data. Take extra care with them.
2. **Share on a need-to-know basis.** Share only with people who need it for their job, and only what they need. Check who is asking. If a caller asks about a colleague or a customer, offer to pass on a message. Send requests from the police and other official bodies to the DPO.
3. **Spot requests and complaints.** People may ask to see, correct or delete their data, or ask us to stop using it. They may complain about how we used it. They can use any words and any channel. Pass it to the DPO the same day. Do not send any data yourself.
4. **Report breaches and near misses at once.** Use the usual reporting route and say it involves personal data. For example: the wrong recipient, lost papers or a lost device, data seen by someone who should not see it, data changed or lost. Do not wait to be sure. Do not try to fix it alone, and do not delete anything. Then do what the DPO asks.
5. **Thinking of a new use for personal data?** Ask the DPO first.
6. **Finished with it?** Keep it only as long as the retention schedule says. Then dispose of it securely (core_06).

## Misconceptions and outdated advice

| Belief | The current position |
|---|---|
| "It's only personal data if it has a name on it." | Personal data is about someone identified *or identifiable*, directly or indirectly (UK GDPR Art 4(1); ICO, "What is personal data?"). A few ordinary details can single someone out. |
| "Special category just means sensitive, like bank details." | It's a fixed list: race or ethnicity, politics, religion or beliefs, union membership, genetics, biometrics for identification, health, sex life or sexual orientation (UK GDPR Art 9(1)). Bank details need protecting but aren't on it. |
| "Work email addresses aren't personal data." | A name with a work email address is personal data (ICO). |
| "Data protection means I can't share anything." | Share what's needed with people who need it. Sharing to prevent crime or to safeguard someone is lawful through the right route (UK GDPR Annex 1, recognised legitimate interests, in force from 5 February 2026). |
| "A subject access request has to be in writing and use the right words." | It can be spoken, written or posted on social media, to anyone, in any words (ICO, "How do we recognise a subject access request?"). |
| "They have to tell us why they want their data." | Their reason doesn't affect whether the request is valid (ICO, right of access guidance). |
| "If the police ask, we have to tell them." | The law allows disclosure to prevent or detect crime; it doesn't require you to answer a phone call. The DPO decides, usually on a written request whose source has been checked (Data Protection Act 2018, Schedule 2, paragraph 2). |
| "The wrong person deleted it, so it isn't a breach." | It's still a breach. It must be recorded, and the DPO decides whether anyone needs telling (UK GDPR Art 33(5)). |
| "I should be sure before I report, so I don't waste anyone's time." | Report at once; the DPO judges it. The organisation may have only 72 hours (UK GDPR Art 33(1)), and near misses are welcome (ICO, "Personal data breaches: a guide"). |
| "Data breaches are cyber attacks." | Human error is the leading cause of reported breaches (ICO, "Personal data breaches: a guide"). |
| "I'll be fined personally if I make a mistake." | Fines are for organisations. Honest mistakes are not offences; deliberately or recklessly taking or sharing data without permission can be (Data Protection Act 2018, s.170). |
| "Keep everything, just in case." | Keep personal data only as long as it's needed, as the retention schedule says (UK GDPR Art 5(1)(e), storage limitation). |
| "Once someone has died, their information doesn't matter." | It isn't personal data under the UK GDPR (ICO), but it can still be confidential, and it often holds details of living relatives. Treat it with care. |

## Real cases

Use these as background and inspiration only. Game content uses fictional names, organisations and figures.

- **Police Service of Northern Ireland, 2023.** On 8 August 2023 a freedom of information response was published on the WhatDoTheyKnow website with an Excel file. The person preparing it had deleted the worksheet tabs they could see, not knowing that a hidden source worksheet behind a pivot table, holding the raw workforce data, was still out of view. It exposed the surnames, initials, ranks or grades, roles and work locations of all 9,483 officers and staff for about 2 hours 20 minutes. The PSNI phoned the ICO about an hour after it found out, the same evening. Officers and staff later described fears for their own and their families' safety, changes to home security and the need to move house. The ICO fined the PSNI £750,000 (penalty notice dated 26 September 2024), reduced from £5.6 million under its approach to public sector fines. Lessons: ordinary work, a document with more in it than anyone knew (core_06 teaches the checks), and a fast report that still couldn't undo publication. Source: the ICO penalty notice (primary).
- **56 Dean Street, 2015.** A newsletter from this London sexual health clinic was emailed to nearly 800 patients of its HIV services with every recipient's name and email address visible to the others. Chelsea and Westminster Hospital NHS Foundation Trust was fined £180,000 in May 2016. Lesson: the visible address list revealed something about every recipient (that they used the clinic's HIV services) without a word of the newsletter itself being sensitive. Source: secondary (Wikipedia's "56 Dean Street" article, citing BBC News, 6 May 2016); check the ICO's penalty notice before quoting the figures to a customer.
- **Ministry of Defence, relocation email, 2021.** On 20 September 2021 an email about the Afghan Relocations and Assistance Policy was sent with the recipients' addresses visible in the To field, exposing people eligible for relocation to the UK. The ICO fined the Ministry of Defence £350,000 (announced 13 December 2023), reduced from an initial £1 million under the ICO's approach to public sector fines. A separate and much larger Ministry of Defence incident (a spreadsheet sent out by mistake in early 2022, made public in July 2025) is covered in core_06. Source: ICO news release, 13 December 2023 (primary).

The ICO's breach guidance also gives a general picture: human error is the leading cause of reported data breaches. (The ICO publishes breach trend data on its website; check the current dashboard before quoting any figure.)

## Glossary

- **Personal data:** any information about a living person who is identified or could be identified, directly or indirectly.
- **Special category data:** personal data about race or ethnicity, political opinions, religion or beliefs, union membership, genetics, biometrics used to identify someone, health, sex life or sexual orientation. Needs extra care.
- **Criminal offence data:** personal data about convictions, offences, allegations, proceedings or sentences.
- **Data subject:** the person the data is about.
- **Controller:** the organisation that decides why and how personal data is used.
- **Processor:** an organisation that handles personal data on a controller's behalf.
- **DPO (Data Protection Officer):** the person who advises the organisation on data protection and handles requests, complaints and breaches.
- **The ICO:** the UK's data protection regulator, the Information Commission (until 30 September 2026, the Information Commissioner).
- **UK GDPR and the Data Protection Act 2018:** the UK's main data protection laws, amended by the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025.
- **Subject access request (SAR):** a person asking for a copy of their own personal data. Any words, any channel.
- **Personal data breach:** personal data lost, destroyed, changed, made unavailable, or seen or sent where it shouldn't be.
- **Near miss:** something that nearly caused a breach. Report it too.
- **Need to know:** sharing only with people who need the information for their work, and only what they need.
- **Retention schedule:** the organisation's list of how long each kind of record is kept.
- **Pseudonymised data:** data with names replaced by codes, where the key exists somewhere. Still personal data.
- **Redaction:** removing information from a copy of a document before it is shared (how to do it properly is in core_06).
- **Safeguarding:** protecting children and adults at risk from harm; it has its own reporting route.
