# core_02 concepts: Social engineering beyond email

This file is the source of truth for the module. The game, the explainers, the knowledge check and the reference card must all match it. Email phishing is covered in core_01; this module is everything else.

## Key messages

1. **The scam does not care how it reaches you.** A call, a text, a chat message, a QR code or a knock at the door can all be the same fraud as a phishing email. Judge the request, not the channel.
2. **Anyone can fake a name, a number or a logo.** Caller ID, a familiar display name and a new "it's me" number are all easy to fake. A real-looking number is not proof of who is calling.
3. **Pause, then check back on a route you already trust.** Hang up, close the chat, and ring the person or organisation on a number you already have: the directory, the back of your card, the colleague in the building. Never the number or link in the message.
4. **Never read out a code, and never give remote access, because someone asked.** Your organisation's real IT will never cold-call you for a one-time code or to install remote-control software. Share a code with no one.
5. **Report it.** Tell your organisation about a suspicious call, text or visitor, even if you already shared something. At home, forward scam texts to 7726, use 159 for calls claiming to be your bank, and report fraud to Report Fraud (Police Scotland on 101 in Scotland).

## Concepts

### The channels

- **Vishing (voice phishing):** a phone call. The caller claims to be IT, your bank, a supplier, a delivery firm, the police or a senior colleague, and pushes you to act now.
- **Smishing (SMS phishing):** a text message, usually with a link: a missed parcel, a fine, a bank alert, a "your account is suspended".
- **Messaging-app impersonation:** a message on a chat app from someone you know, often from a new number: "Hi, it's me, I've lost my phone, can you do me a quick favour?" The "Hi Mum" family version and the "boss who needs gift cards" work version are the same trick.
- **Quishing (QR phishing):** a QR code that leads to a fake site. QR codes turn up on stickers over real ones (car park machines are the most reported place), on posters, in letters and in emails. A QR code hides where the link really goes.
- **Pretexting in person:** a visitor with a believable story, a uniform or a clipboard, who talks their way into information or access. Here the focus is the conversation and what they ask for; the building and the door are core_10.
- **Tech support scams:** an unsolicited caller or pop-up says your computer or account has a problem, and offers to "fix" it if you install software or hand over access or a payment.

### The levers

Social engineering works on normal, helpful behaviour, not on stupidity. Over the phone the pressure is sharper than in email because a person is waiting on the line.

| Lever | What it sounds like |
|---|---|
| Authority | "This is IT Security" / "I'm calling from the Chief Executive's office" |
| Urgency | "Your account will be locked in the next few minutes" |
| Fear | "There's been fraud on your account and we need to act now" |
| Helpfulness | "You'd really be helping me out, I'm in a meeting" |
| Flattery | "You sound like someone who knows what they're doing" |
| Insider jargon | Using real names, system names and processes to sound like one of you |

A good caller mixes several, and uses real details (a manager's name, the system you use, a project) picked up from the website, social media (core_12) or an earlier breach, so they sound like an insider.

### Why caller ID and a known number are not proof

Phone numbers and display names can be spoofed, so a call that shows your bank's number, or a text that lands in the same thread as real messages from a sender, can still be fake. A chat message from a "new number" claiming to be a colleague or family member is the same: the account is new, not the person. This is why the defence is never "does the number look right?" but "I will reach them on a number I already have".

### The requests that should always make you pause

- A **one-time code or MFA prompt**: anyone who phones or messages asking you to read out a code, or to approve a prompt, is trying to get into an account. Genuine IT does not need your code.
- **Remote access**: installing a remote-control or "support" tool, or reading out what is on your screen, hands over the device.
- A **payment or a change of bank details** by phone or message (this belongs mainly to core_11; here, do not action it, verify it).
- **Information**: personal data, who does what, security details, when someone is away.
- An **urgent, secret favour**: buy gift cards, move money, send a document, "keep this between us".

### Why checking back works

An attacker controls everything inside the contact they started: the number shown, the voice, the name, the link. They do not control the routes you already trust. If you hang up and dial the number in the staff directory, on the back of your card, or on a real statement, or walk to the colleague's desk, you reach the real person and the scam falls apart. The managed security team in one of our other games does exactly this: it refuses to make a change on an unverified phone request and rings an authorised contact back on the number it already holds. Calling back on a trusted number defeats almost every phone and message scam, which is why it beats trying to judge whether a voice or a number sounds right.

A practical point: call back on a different line or after a clear gap where you can. Some scam calls stay connected on the line even after you think you have hung up, so use a mobile, or wait, or ring a number you can see is ringing out.

### The habit to keep (the shared rule of thumb)

The built episodes end every debrief on one rule of thumb, identical in all shells and enforced by a test. It is the beyond-email habit too, since a call, a text or a QR code is just another way the same pressure arrives. Quote it word for word:

> When a message pressures you to move money, grant access, sign in or read back a code, stop and check it yourself through a route you already trust, not the link, number or page the message gives you. Taking a minute to confirm is always a fair thing to do.

"Pause. Check. Report." is not used.

### One-time codes and remote access

- **Never read a code to anyone.** A code sent to you is for you to type into the real app or site yourself, never to repeat to a caller or messenger. Passwords, MFA codes and one-time passcodes are the keys to your accounts (core_03).
- **MFA fatigue (prompt bombing):** if approval prompts keep arriving when you did not sign in, do not approve one to make them stop. Deny them and report it; someone has your password and is trying to get in.
- **Remote access:** do not install remote-control or "support" software, and do not read out your screen, because a caller asked. If you already did, disconnect the device from the network if you can, and report it at once; the service desk will help.

### QR codes

A QR code is just a link you cannot read. Before you scan one, ask whether you trust where it is and who put it there. Treat a code on a sticker, a poster, a letter or an email the way you would treat a link from a stranger. If you scan one and it asks you to sign in, pay, or give personal details, stop. Check for a sticker placed over the original, and use the organisation's own app or website, typed in yourself, instead. Use your phone's built-in camera or scanner rather than a QR app downloaded from a store.

### At home

The same habits protect personal accounts and money:

- **Scam texts:** forward them to **7726** (free). Your mobile provider can then investigate and block the number. 7726 spells SPAM on a keypad.
- **Calls claiming to be your bank:** hang up and call **159**, a short number that connects you to your own bank's fraud line. Most UK banks take part.
- **Fraud and cyber crime:** report to **Report Fraud** at reportfraud.police.uk or 0300 123 2040 (in England, Wales and Northern Ireland; in Scotland, call Police Scotland on 101).
- **Messaging apps** such as WhatsApp, iMessage and Signal are not covered by 7726: use the block-and-report option inside the app.

At work, use your organisation's reporting route instead, so the security team can warn others.

## What to do (the procedure)

As the reference card shows it:

1. **Pause.** Does the caller or message want a code, a payment, remote access, information or an urgent favour? Are they rushing you, scaring you, or asking you to keep it quiet?
2. **Check back.** Hang up or close the chat. Reach the person or organisation on a number you already have: the directory, the back of your card, a colleague in the building. Never the number or link they gave you.
3. **Never share a code or give remote access.** Genuine IT will not ask.
4. **Report it** through the organisation's route, even if you already shared something. At home: 7726 for texts, 159 for bank calls, Report Fraud for the rest.

## Misconceptions and outdated advice

| Belief | The current position |
|---|---|
| "The caller ID showed the real number, so it was genuine." | Numbers and display names are easily spoofed. Judge the request and check back. |
| "It was a new number but they knew things only my colleague would know." | Attackers research people from public posts and past breaches. A new "it's me" number is a warning sign, not proof. |
| "IT asked for my code, so it must be fine." | Real IT never needs your one-time code. Nobody does. Do not read it out. |
| "I let them onto my screen but didn't type anything, so no harm done." | Remote access alone can be enough. Disconnect and report. |
| "QR codes are safe; it's just a menu." | A QR code hides its link. On a sticker, poster, letter or email, treat it like a link from a stranger. |
| "I should report it only if I lost money." | Report every attempt. Early reports let the team warn everyone and may stop the next person losing money. |
| "I hung up, so the line is clear to call the bank." | Some scam calls hold the line. Use another phone, or wait, or call a number you can see ringing out. |

## Real cases

Use as background and inspiration only. Game content uses fictional names.

- **Retool, 2023.** An employee received a text claiming to be from IT about an HR benefits (open enrolment) problem, signed in on a fake page, then took a phone call from a convincing "IT" caller and shared a one-time code; the attacker enrolled their own device and reached customer accounts. (Retool blog, 13 September 2023.) The voice-cloning element of that call belongs to core_15; the vishing and code-sharing belong here.
- **Scattered Spider, 2022 onward.** A joint US advisory (CISA and FBI, AA23-320A, first published 16 November 2023, updated 29 July 2025) describes a group that phoned and texted staff and help desks posing as IT to get passwords, have remote-access tools installed, and have staff read out MFA codes, and that used repeated MFA prompts (push bombing) and SIM swaps.
- **Twitter, July 2020.** Attackers phoned staff posing as the company's own IT support, used a VPN problem as the pretext, sent people to a fake sign-in page and captured passwords and MFA codes in real time, then reached internal tools. (Widely reported; New York Department of Financial Services report, October 2020. Exact wording to verify.)
- **"Hi Mum" and "new number" scams.** UK police and consumer bodies have repeatedly warned about messages from a supposed family member or colleague on a new number asking for urgent money or a favour. (Which? and Action Fraud warnings, 2022 onward; cite the current edition before quoting any figure.)
- **Quishing in car parks, 2024 to 2025.** Action Fraud reported 784 "quishing" reports in the year to April 2025, with almost 3.5 million pounds lost, and said car parks were the most common place, with fake stickers placed over genuine QR codes on payment machines. (Action Fraud alert, June 2025.)

## Glossary

- **Social engineering:** tricking a person into doing something that helps an attacker, by exploiting trust, helpfulness or pressure.
- **Vishing:** phishing by voice call.
- **Smishing:** phishing by text message.
- **Quishing:** phishing using a QR code.
- **Pretexting:** inventing a believable story and role to get information or access.
- **Spoofing:** faking the number, name or address that a call or message appears to come from.
- **One-time code / OTP:** a short code sent to you to confirm a sign-in; for you to type in, never to share.
- **MFA fatigue / prompt bombing:** flooding someone with approval prompts hoping they approve one.
- **Remote-access tool:** software that lets someone else control your device.
- **7726, 159:** UK short numbers to report scam texts and to reach your bank's fraud line.
