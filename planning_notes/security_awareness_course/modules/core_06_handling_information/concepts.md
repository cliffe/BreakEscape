# core_06 concepts: Handling information

This file is the source of truth for the module. The game, the explainers, the knowledge check and the reference card must all match it. It was checked on 14 October 2026.

Boundaries: what personal data is, people's rights, and what counts as a personal data breach are taught in core_05, which this file points to (the wording about breaches below is the same). Cloud tools, personal accounts and USB sticks are core_13; clear desk and clear screen are core_10 (this module covers only printing and disposal); printing at home is core_09.

## Key messages

1. **Every piece of information has a label, and the label tells you how to handle it:** who can see it, how to send it, where to keep it and how to get rid of it. No label? Judge it by what's in it, and ask.
2. **Need to know.** Share with the named people who need it, only what they need, for as long as they need it.
3. **Check before it leaves:** every recipient, every link setting, and what's hidden inside the file.
4. **Confidential paper goes in the locked confidential waste, not the recycling. Old devices go back to IT. Collect your printing straight away.**
5. **If something goes wrong, or you receive something you shouldn't have, report it at once.** If it involves personal data, it may be a personal data breach (core_05).

## Concepts

### Why handling matters

- Most information leaks through everyday handling rather than hacking. CIS Control 14.5 lists the usual causes: sending data to the wrong person, losing a portable device, and publishing data to unintended audiences.
- The harm isn't only to people's privacy. A leaked price can lose a contract; a leaked floor plan or alarm code can help a burglar; a leaked board paper can move a share price or end a deal. Leaked personal data harms people (core_05).
- Attackers collect what organisations throw away or leave open: paper in recycling bins, links that anyone can open, files with hidden content. They need little skill for any of it.

### Classification and labels

Larkspur uses four labels. Customers can rename them and set their own definitions (BE-05).

| Label | What it means | Examples | Handling in one line |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Public** | Approved for anyone to see | The brochure, published tender notices, job adverts, the website | Publish only the approved version |
| **Internal** | For Larkspur staff and trusted contractors; little harm if it got out | The canteen menu, most procedures, the internal phone list | Keep it inside Larkspur; don't post it publicly |
| **Confidential** | Would harm Larkspur, its clients or people if it got out; need to know only | Pricing and bids, contracts, most personal data (customer claims, staff records), incident reports | Named people only; secure send outside Larkspur; secure print; locked confidential waste |
| **Restricted** | Serious harm if it got out; a few named people | Board papers on a takeover, special category data and large sets of personal data, security details (alarm codes, network diagrams), HR investigations | A named list; the approved secure route only; avoid printing; never off site without approval |

- **Who labels?** The person who creates the information labels it when they create it, and anyone who receives unlabelled information labels it when they save it. Labels go in the header or footer, in the email subject or sensitivity label, and in the file name where that helps.
- **No label?** Judge it by what's in it. Anything with personal data is at least Confidential. If unsure, ask the owner, and in the meantime treat it as the higher label.
- **Don't over-label.** If everything is Restricted, the label stops meaning anything and people start working around it.
- **Public sector clients use the government's own scheme** (OFFICIAL, SECRET and TOP SECRET). OFFICIAL information can carry a "-SENSITIVE" marking, which means extra controls and stricter need to know (Government Security Classifications, "Working at OFFICIAL"). Follow the client's marking and any handling instructions; at Larkspur, treat OFFICIAL-SENSITIVE as at least Confidential.

### Need to know

- Share with people who need the information for their work, only what they need, and only for as long as they need it.
- Need isn't rank. A director without a reason doesn't need the HR file; the person returning a cardigan does need the room number.
- It applies inside the organisation too: not everyone at Larkspur needs the bid pricing.
- Holding back what a colleague genuinely needs is a failure too. The aim is the right information to the right people.

### Sending

- **Check every recipient before you press send.** The usual slips are autocomplete picking the wrong person, reply-all, a long thread with outsiders copied in, and a forwarded chain with old messages underneath.
- **Use the safety features your mail client has.** The ICO suggests organisations consider turning off autocomplete for recipients, adding a short delay before emails leave so mistakes can be caught, and warning people when they use the CC field. Many mail systems can also flag outside recipients. Where these exist, don't switch them off.
- **Emails to a group.** Addresses in To or CC are visible to everyone. Bcc hides them, but it is easy to forget, and the ICO says it is "not enough on its own" to protect people's information. For mailings to many people, or where being on the list says something about a person (patients of a clinic, families of care home residents), use the bulk email tool or mail merge so each person gets their own email.
- **Confidential outside Larkspur:** use the "Send securely" option (encryption) or the approved file-sharing service. **Restricted:** only by the approved secure route.
- **Password-protected files:** send the password another way (a phone call or a text), never in the same email. A key sent with the lock protects nothing; the ICO's encryption guidance makes the same point about keeping keys apart from the data.
- **Personal accounts:** never send work information to a personal email or personal cloud account, even your own (core_13 covers the reasons and the approved alternatives).
- **Send only what's needed.** Take out what the recipient doesn't need before it goes (core_05).

### Sharing links and folders

- **Choose "specific people", not "anyone with the link".** A link works for whoever has it, and links get forwarded.
- **Give the lowest permission that works:** view or comment rather than edit.
- **Set an end date** for anything shared outside Larkspur.
- **Sharing a folder shares everything in it,** including files added later.
- **Review what you've shared.** Remove access when the work ends. If you find a link that's open to anyone and holds Confidential information or personal data, close it and report it.

### Hidden content: is it really gone?

Files carry more than you can see. The ICO's guidance on disclosing documents securely lists the main traps:

- **Spreadsheets:** hidden worksheets, rows and columns; worksheets whose tabs are simply out of view (the PSNI case in core_05); active filters; pivot tables, which keep a copy of their source data; links to other workbooks. Hiding something isn't removing it, and the ICO advises against hiding rows, columns or worksheets as a way to protect information.
- **Documents:** comments, tracked changes, hidden text (white on white, or under a shape), metadata such as the author and earlier titles, embedded files, and photos that record where they were taken.
- **Redaction done wrongly:** a black box or highlight drawn over text leaves the text underneath, ready to copy; changing the font colour hides nothing; blurring can be reversed; black marker on paper can sometimes be read against the light.
- **The checks:** work on a copy; use the redaction tool, which removes the text; use the document inspection tool, then look yourself (tools miss some things, such as colour-hidden text); unhide everything; save as a simpler format (CSV or plain text) to see what's really there, remembering that converting to PDF alone isn't enough; check the file size is what you'd expect; for anything important, ask a colleague to check it too.

### Storing

- Keep work information in the approved places: the shared drive, the document system and the approved file-sharing service.
- Not on the desktop, in downloads, on a personal device or USB stick (core_13), or in a personal email account.
- Paper marked Confidential or Restricted is locked away when you're not using it (clear desk is core_10).

### Printing

- **Print only what you need,** and avoid printing Restricted information.
- **Use secure print:** the job waits until you release it at the printer with your badge or PIN, so nobody else picks it up first.
- **Collect it straight away,** and check you have all your pages and nobody else's.
- **Found someone else's printout?** Don't read it. Take it to the owner (the name is on the cover sheet) or their team. If it holds personal data and has been sitting in the open, make sure it's reported (core_05). If nobody claims it, it goes in the locked confidential waste.

### Disposal and retention

- **Paper:** everything marked Internal or above goes in the locked confidential waste consoles, which an approved contractor collects and destroys. Never put it in ordinary recycling, which anyone handling the bins can read.
- **Shredders:** shredded paper can be pieced back together (see the real cases). At Larkspur the strip-cut shredder by the printer is for Internal paper only; Confidential and Restricted paper goes in the locked console.
- **Devices:** old laptops, phones, tablets and memory sticks go back to IT, which wipes or destroys them properly. Deleting files isn't enough: the NCSC's guidance on sanitising storage media says "Simply hitting the 'Delete' key isn't enough." Never bin, sell or give away a work device.
- **Files:** delete what you no longer need when the retention schedule says, including your own copies in downloads, on the desktop and in email attachments.
- **Whiteboards** in meeting rooms are wiped after use (core_10).

### Information you shouldn't have

- A misdirected email, a competitor's document, someone else's personal data, a confidential printout on a shared printer: don't open it further, don't use it and don't forward it. Tell the sender, report it (to the security team, and to the DPO if it involves personal data), and delete it when you're told to.
- In public tenders it matters even more. Under the Procurement Act 2023 a public body can exclude a supplier that has acted improperly in a procurement, including by accessing confidential information that gives it an unfair advantage (Schedule 7, paragraph 13).

### When it goes wrong

Report it at once through the usual route: a link set to "anyone", an email to the wrong person, a lost printout, a missing device, a file sent with hidden content. If personal data is involved, it may be a personal data breach, which the DPO must judge quickly because the organisation may have only 72 hours to tell the ICO (core_05). Don't wait to be sure, don't try to fix it alone, and nobody is in trouble for reporting.

## What to do (the procedure)

As the reference card shows it, with the organisation's own labels and tools filled in:

1. **Label it** when you create it: Public, Internal, Confidential or Restricted. No label on something you've received? Judge it by what's in it, and ask.
2. **Need to know:** the named people who need it, only what they need, for as long as they need it.
3. **Check before you send:** every recipient (watch autocomplete and reply-all); a group mailing goes through the bulk email tool or mail merge, not To or CC; Confidential outside Larkspur goes by "Send securely"; a password goes by phone or text, never in the same email; never to a personal account.
4. **Share with names, not links:** specific people, the lowest permission that works, an end date. Review and remove access when the work ends.
5. **Is it really gone?** Before a file leaves, check for hidden worksheets, rows and columns, comments and tracked changes, and covered-up text. Redact with the redaction tool, on a copy, and check the result.
6. **Print and pick up:** secure print, collect at once. Someone else's printout goes back to them unread.
7. **Dispose properly:** paper marked Internal or above in the locked confidential waste; devices back to IT; files deleted when the retention schedule says.
8. **Something gone wrong, or received something you shouldn't have?** Don't use or forward it. Report it at once through the usual route.

## Misconceptions and outdated advice

| Belief | The current position |
|---|---|
| "Bcc solves the group email problem." | Bcc hides addresses but is easy to forget and "not enough on its own"; use bulk email or mail merge for large or sensitive mailings (ICO, "Email and security"). |
| "If I hide the worksheet, nobody will see it." | Hidden worksheets, rows and columns are still in the file; remove them, don't hide them (ICO, checklist for spreadsheets). |
| "A black box over the text redacts it." | The text is still underneath and can be copied; use a redaction tool and check the result (ICO, "How do we avoid an accidental breach when redacting information?"). |
| "Saving it as a PDF strips out anything hidden." | Converting to PDF removes some metadata but is not a secure redaction method on its own (ICO, redaction guidance). |
| "Shredded means destroyed." | Shredded documents have been pieced back together, by hand and by computer (Tehran, 1979; DARPA Shredder Challenge, 2011). Use the locked confidential waste for Confidential paper. |
| "I deleted the files, so the old laptop is clean." | Deleting isn't enough; devices need proper sanitising (NCSC, "Secure sanitisation of storage media"). Give them back to IT. |
| "Putting the password in the same email is fine; the file's still protected." | The key travels with the lock. Send it another way (ICO encryption guidance: keys kept with the data are as good as no encryption). |
| "'Anyone with the link' only means the people I sent it to." | It means anyone who ever gets the link, including everyone it's forwarded to. Share with specific people. |
| "If in doubt, label it Restricted." | Over-labelling makes labels meaningless and pushes people to work around them. Label by what's in it, and ask the owner. |
| "It's internal email, so it doesn't matter what I attach." | Internal recipients still need a reason to see it: need to know applies inside the organisation too. |
| "If it came to me by mistake, it's not my problem." | You now hold information you shouldn't. Don't use or forward it; tell the sender and report it (and in a tender, using it can get the bid excluded: Procurement Act 2023, Schedule 7, paragraph 13). |

## Real cases

Use these as background and inspiration only. Game content uses fictional names, organisations and figures. core_05 has the main personal data cases (the PSNI spreadsheet, 56 Dean Street); this module adds handling cases.

- **Ministry of Defence spreadsheet, early 2022 (made public July 2025).** A defence official sent out a spreadsheet by mistake. The official meant to send details of about 150 people, but the file held over 33,000 entries relating to about 18,700 Afghans who had applied to come to the UK. It went unnoticed until August 2023, when an extract appeared on Facebook, and a court injunction kept it secret until July 2025. Lesson: check what is actually in an attachment before it leaves. Source: secondary (Wikipedia, "Afghan Response Route", summarising July 2025 reporting); check the government's own statements before quoting figures.
- **The ICO's own email examples.** The ICO's "Email and security" guidance describes two cases it fined, without naming the organisations: in 2019 an NHS trust emailed patients about an art competition with their addresses copied into the To field, and a recall attempt failed; in February 2020 a charity sent an event agenda to 105 members with addresses in CC rather than Bcc, and 65 of the addresses identified the recipients. Source: primary (ICO).
- **Tehran, 1979.** Shredded documents from the US embassy, seized in November 1979, were painstakingly reconstructed and published in a series of volumes. Source: secondary (Wikipedia, "Iran hostage crisis").
- **DARPA Shredder Challenge, 2011.** Nearly 9,000 teams competed to rebuild shredded documents; the winning team of eight reconstructed five documents from more than 10,000 pieces, in about 600 hours of work. Lesson: a determined person can rebuild shreds. Source: secondary (Wikipedia, "DARPA Shredder Challenge 2011", citing DARPA's announcement).
