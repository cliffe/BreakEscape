# Concept pitches

Five concepts for the children's game. They are not mutually exclusive: the strongest final product probably takes the world of one and the mascot of another. Each pitch says what it is, why a child would play it, what it leans on from the engine, and its risks. All follow the vision and safeguarding rules in `00_VISION_AND_SAFEGUARDING.md`.

A quick way to read them: concepts 1 and 2 are the two names the brief suggested; concepts 3 to 5 are alternatives that avoid their naming risks.

---

## 1. Crypto Cat

**One line:** a friendly cat who guards secrets helps you keep yours safe, across a town of everyday online moments.

**Mascot and framing:** Crypto Cat is the guide (the children's version of the awareness mentor): warm, a bit silly, never preachy. "Crypto" is played as "keeper of secrets" (a cat who loves hiding things), with a padlock collar. The child plays themselves, with Crypto Cat along for help.

**World:** a small cartoon town the child moves around, top-down, exactly like a Break Escape mission: your bedroom with a tablet, the school computer room, a friend's house, the games den, the park. Each place holds one or two short adventures.

**Sample adventures:**
- **The Password Den:** build a strong passphrase by picking three random picture-words (banana, rocket, dragon), and learn why a pet's name is easy to guess. (Password builder minigame.)
- **The Mystery Message:** a message says "You have won a free game, tap here!". Stop, think, check with a grown-up. (A kid-safe inbox or messaging panel.)
- **Who Can See This?:** set who can see a photo you want to post; discover a stranger could see it, and fix it. (Sharing-settings panel.)
- **Is It Really Them?:** a new "friend" in a game says they are also nine and asks where you go to school. Learn that people online are not always who they say, and tell a trusted adult. (Dialogue choice, no scary content.)

**Why a child plays it:** a cute collectable mascot, a town to explore, badges for Crypto Cat's collar.

**Leans on:** the whole engine (rooms, movement, person-chat), the mentor-explainer pattern, the password, sharing and messaging minigames, assessed decisions and the debrief, badges.

**Risks:** the name (see the naming note below) and that a single cute mascot can feel young for the top of KS2. Best fit 7 to 9.

## 2. Hacker Kids (reframed as defenders)

**One line:** a club of kids who protect their school and friends from online tricks, learning how the tricks work so they can stop them.

**Framing caution:** as a *safety* game we do not want children practising attacks or thinking "hacker" means breaking in. So the club are **defenders and good online citizens** ("white hats", explained simply as "the good kind who help keep people safe"). The attacker's-eye content that the awareness course uses for adults (the pretext workshop, MG-30) does **not** belong in a young children's game.

**World:** a school club room is the hub. Each week a problem arrives (a rumour spreading in the class chat, a pop-up on the library computer, a friend who overshared) and the club solves it together. Recurring kid characters give it a cast and continuity.

**Sample episodes:**
- **The Spreading Rumour:** someone forwarded an unkind message. Learn not to pass it on, to be kind, and to tell a teacher. (Kindness and bullying strand.)
- **The Library Pop-up:** "Your computer has a virus, call this number!". Recognise a scary trick, do not tap, tell an adult. (Spot-the-risk plus browser panel.)
- **The Too-Good Prize:** free in-game coins if you type your parent's card number. Stop and check. (Scams and money.)
- **Trick Spotter:** across episodes, the club keeps a "trick cards" collection (urgency, "you have won!", pretending to be someone) and ticks one off each time they spot it. (A simplified tactic spotter card.)

**Why a child plays it:** belonging to a club, a cast of friends, levelling up from "cub" to "captain", solving a new case each time.

**Leans on:** rooms and cast, the tactic spotter card (simplified), spot-the-risk, the browser and messaging panels, role-model characters who report.

**Risks:** the word "hacker" in the title worries some parents and schools and slightly misframes a safety game; best kept as a working name. Best fit 9 to 13 (upper KS2 and KS3).

## 3. SafeSquad (recommended lead framing)

**One line:** you and a squad of friendly characters earn belts by keeping your corner of the internet safe.

**Framing:** a defender-positive squad with one guide character (a mascot can still be Crypto Cat) and a few kid teammates, each a bit good at one thing (one is kind, one is careful, one is brave about telling an adult). This takes the warmth of Crypto Cat and the club belonging of Hacker Kids, without the naming risks of either.

**World:** a hub "den" with doors to themed worlds, one per Connected World strand (Passwords and Secrets, Kind Online, Is It Real?, Private and Proud, Screen Balance). Completing a world earns a coloured belt. Progression is visible and collectable.

**Sample worlds:** as in the Connected World table in `00_VISION_AND_SAFEGUARDING.md`; each world is three to five short beats following the kids beat pattern.

**Why a child plays it:** belts to collect, a squad that grows, clear progress, short sessions that fit a lesson or a bedtime.

**Leans on:** everything the others do, plus the awareness course's pathway idea (a set order of worlds, like Induction Essentials) and its badge and record design.

**Risks:** "SafeSquad" may already be used (trademark and search check needed). Best fit the full 7 to 11 core, extensible to KS3.

## 4. Byte-Size Heroes

**One line:** tiny heroes who live in your devices and show you how to keep them (and you) safe.

**Framing:** a shrink-down conceit: the heroes live inside the tablet, the console and the smart speaker and take the child on quick quests about the device in front of them. Good for teaching device settings, updates, permissions and "what does this app want to know about me?" in a concrete, visual way.

**Sample quests:** turn on the lock screen; decide whether a game really needs your location; notice an app asking for the camera; set up the simple two-step "two keys are better than one" check.

**Why a child plays it:** the inside-the-device world is novel and visual; quests are bite-size (the name promises it).

**Leans on:** the device-settings minigame most of all, plus MFA, sharing settings and the spot-the-risk mode.

**Risks:** narrower (device-centric) than the others, so better as a *world within* SafeSquad than a whole game on its own. Best fit 8 to 12.

---

## 5. Cyber Cubs (early-years leaning)

**One line:** a den of animal cubs learn the online-safety basics through songs, stickers and very short activities.

**Framing:** the youngest-skewing option (5 to 7, KS1), audio-first, almost no reading, three or four activities and a lot of repetition and praise. Animal cubs rather than human kids so the youngest players see themselves in it.

**Sample activities:** "secret or share?" sorting (is this okay to tell everyone?); "tell a grown-up" practice; a simple password song.

**Why a child plays it:** gentle, colourful, sticker rewards, parent-and-child together.

**Leans on:** the engine's audio and the simplest sorting and choice minigames; the classification sorter reskinned as "secret or share".

**Risks:** a different, younger product with its own art and a much simpler engine use; best treated as a later companion, not the first build. Best fit 5 to 7.

## Comparing the five

| Concept | Best age | Original design needed | Engine reuse | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Crypto Cat | 7 to 9 | Low | Very high | Name (crypto-coin read); feels young at the top |
| Hacker Kids | 9 to 13 | Medium | High | "Hacker" framing worries buyers |
| **SafeSquad** | **7 to 11 (+KS3)** | **Medium** | **Very high** | **Name availability** |
| Byte-Size Heroes | 8 to 12 | Medium | High (device-led) | Narrow; better as a world |
| Cyber Cubs | 5 to 7 | High (new art, simpler engine) | Medium | A separate younger product |

**Recommendation:** build **one game with the SafeSquad framing** (defender-positive, belts, a squad and a guide), **keep Crypto Cat as the guide mascot** inside it, fold **Byte-Size Heroes** in as the device world, and hold **Cyber Cubs** as a possible early-years companion later. "Hacker Kids" can be a marketing sub-brand for the upper-KS3 episodes if the defender framing is kept. This gets the warmth, the belonging and the collectability of all the strong ideas into one product that reuses the most engine and awareness-course work.

## Naming note

The brief named "Crypto Cat" and "Hacker Kids". Before any name is chosen:

- **Run a trademark, domain and app-store search** on every candidate (as `DECISIONS_PENDING` D2 requires for the awareness course). "Crypto Cat", "SafeSquad", "Cyber Cubs" and "Byte-Size Heroes" are the kind of names likely to be taken somewhere.
- **"Crypto Cat"** will be read as cryptocurrency by many adults in 2026, and there is an existing "Cryptocat" secure-chat app; it also risks confusion with crypto-coin marketing aimed at children, which we do not want.
- **"Hacker Kids"** frames children as attackers. Keep a defender-positive public name; "hacker" can stay as an internal working name or an older-age sub-brand only.
- A name should be easy for a seven-year-old to say and spell, and reassuring to the adult who buys it. Shortlist to take forward: **SafeSquad**, **Cyber Cubs**, **Guardians** (as in "guardians of the password"), with Crypto Cat as the mascot's name rather than the product's.
