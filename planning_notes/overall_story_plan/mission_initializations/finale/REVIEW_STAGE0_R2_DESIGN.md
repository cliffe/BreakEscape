## 3. The reworked design, judged fresh

**The Publication logic.** The best idea in the finale, and it now holds. "Only a SAFETYNET hand proves the point" explains the invitation, the keyed door, the waiting, the unopposed stop and the Join offer in one motive, and it is a teacher's vanity, which suits him. The 06:00 fallback keeps the threat real without letting it decide anything. It needs three repairs: the sleeper (R2-03), the cells' head start (R2-08), and the clock's effect on the climax (R2-02). Once he pauses the clock for 0x00, the motive shows itself in play rather than in exposition.

**The sleeper whodunit.** It is fair, and on the current text it is too easy and over too soon. One log entry names her (R2-04). Fairness is not the problem: the evidence is always enough, and HaX nudges. The problem is that it lacks the middle that makes a whodunit fun: suspicion, an alibi, a clue the player holds in their own inventory. A **wrong accusation's cost** is currently both invisible and identical to not accusing (R2-06). The proposed scale (an innocent's one line, a lost team function, the sleeper acting at the core, recall possible) is proportionate. It stings, never blocks, and never kills. The m09 route that detains her at the briefing is a strong reward for solving it early; keep it.

**The core sequence.** Case, then answers, then console, then database, then his fate. The order is right, and the gates are right: lethal force only at the dead-man switch, and Join only from "Show me how it works" with three steps. Two weaknesses: the talk-down can't be lost by a player who is trying (R2-15), and the climax may sit on 30 minutes of VM work (R2-02). The dead-man moment is well judged: firing is never needed (HaX can kill the feed), so the lethal ending is a choice about trust, not survival. Make sure the narration never implies otherwise.

**Scorched Earth as his own purge.** Works. "The one outcome where he can't win the argument" is a good reason, the escape follows from it, and the epilogue states the cost plainly. Netherton's suspension line is still one of the best lines in the plan.

**m09's clock and the choice for everyone.** The Recruiter's offer gives every player a real moment, and on the m05-sold branch the tell-or-hang-up beat is the best-built carry-over in m09. The purge is the right idea, run the wrong way (R2-10). Event-driven, visible, and with one save-this-or-that choice, it becomes a second choice for everyone and stops costing VM learners.

**The disclosure theme taught through play.** Much better than round 1. The player finds the kept flaw before he argues it, sees the schedule's victims by city, and does the m07-style triage. The remaining gap is that coordinated disclosure, the answer the course wants students to understand, is never *done* by the player (R2-12). One optional act in m09, answered in m10, fixes that cheaply.

**Five full returning scenes in ~100 minutes.** The count and the time are right (about 12–15 minutes of the 100 on reunions). Who gets them is the problem (R2-01), along with whether the player reaches them (R2-13) and Ghost's weight (R2-21).

**Nine rooms each.** Fine for both. m09's nine all have a job. In m10, the media room is dead on one branch (R2-18), and the plant room needs a reason to visit (R2-13). Nine rooms with five scenes, a whodunit and two optional tasks is about m02's density, which shipped.

**HaX's arc.** Well placed in the scenes: the confession in m09, the line's meaning told to the player, the overheard call, the jam, the last word. What it lacks is a turn the player causes (R2-19). One exported answer to her confession and one moment at the core where the player hands her the floor would make her the season's emotional centre rather than its narrator.

**The cold-start "previously on".** The wall of cards is the right device for m09, and the "who is Tesseract" beat is necessary. Keep the *spoken* walk to four or five cards and let the rest sit on the wall, or it becomes a roll-call for a player who played everything. m10 needs its own (R2-11), and the never-played cards must match the defaults (R2-16).

## 4. Payoff assessment

**Will a player with strong m01–m08 choices notice the payoffs at the right moments?** Mostly yes, and the timing is good. The big ones land at moments of decision rather than in a debrief: the Recruiter's receipt during her call, Nightshade's wrong entry at the door, Victoria's offer at the outer lock, the m04 choice in the first line of Tesseract's case. Two things stop it being a clear yes. First, the careful player's wins mostly arrive as invisible help (a map pin, a shorter cross-reference, an empty guard post), with no person in the room (R2-01). Second, several Tier 1 helpers change something the player never knew could be otherwise. A helper only counts as felt if someone says out loud that it is there because of what the player did. Every R/H import needs its HaX or NPC line naming the cause, at the moment it helps.

### The five most memorable payoffs

1. **Nightshade at the door** (rows 104–106). He pays in full, one entry is wrong, and which way he tips depends on whether the player sent him his own recruitment file in m09. A choice in m08, a choice in m09 and a consequence in m10, all in one scene.
2. **The Recruiter's receipt** (row 61). On the m05-sold branch she opens with "Does your handler know?" while HaX is on the line. The player chooses there and then, and Tesseract uses the answer against HaX in m10. Three missions, and the player's own secret.
3. **The m04 disclosure choice landing twice** (row 48). The player finds the flaw SAFETYNET kept (m09), then hears their own Albion call as the first line of Tesseract's case. On `full`, it becomes their best argument back.
4. **Victoria in the gallery** (row 32). m03's "whether she stays turned is anyone's guess" is answered by a choice the player makes with evidence from the schedule. A recruitment the player made two-thirds of a season ago finally gets tested.
5. **"Let it hurt afterwards, not during"** (row 120). Planted in m07, explained by HaX in m09, finished by the player to Tesseract's face in m10. The season's line becomes the player's argument.

Close behind: Voltage stood down by his own print; the dispatch count ticking since day nine for a player who left Austin uncovered; the blast door that opens for 0x00 by name.

### The three weakest Tier 1 items

1. **`m06_assets_seized`** (m10, row 72). Two empty guard posts and a gap in the fence. An absence is the hardest thing in a game to feel: a player who never saw the full staffing won't notice two missing guards. Tesseract's line helps, but it comes at the end. Fix: HaX at the fence, the moment the player arrives: "Two posts empty. That's your freeze. He couldn't make payroll." A broken padlock and a "posts unmanned: budget" memo on the duty board would also do it.
2. **`m05_torres_turned` / `m05_final_choice`** (m09, row 64). One step shorter on an *optional* cross-reference, or one HaX line about the gap. The player's choice in m05 decided a man's life; this payoff is a saved click. Fix: on `torres_turned`, Torres himself rings in (he is in protection), recognises the filing scheme, and says one thing about the forty-seven that only he would know. On `combat_lethal`, his name is in the ledger as a "closed placement", in Sorensen's hand.
3. **`m02_ghost_deal_refused`** (m10, row 15). Ghost gives "the hour the core's guard changes", but nothing in the m10 design uses a guard-change time. So it is a fact with no mechanic. Fix: make the fact open something (the outer lock is unguarded for one window, a second way into the gallery), or fold it into R2-21: Ghost's parley is where the player talks Ghost out of selling the head start.

Runner-up: `m06_monitoring_enabled` (m09). Lines on a map in a cutscene the player doesn't control. Making the tagged payments an object at the drop terminal (the player follows one line to Tomb Beta's lease) would make it felt.

### On the tiering, under the user's "carry everything" rule

The breadth is right, and the Tier 3 cuts are honest (only rows that lost their payoff). The tier line is the problem (R2-09). With that moved, Tier 2's fixed strings are a good cheap way to honour forty-odd small choices, provided each is written as something said *to* the player at a moment, not read *by* the player in a list.

## 5. Fun, difficulty and "what next"

**Is it fun and not hard?** For the most part. Every clock is non-fatal, every carry-over obstacle has a way round, every presence has a default, and the endings can't be reached by accident. Those are the right rules, and the documents keep them consistently. The difficulty risks are all where real time meets the VM: the purge (R2-10) and the climax clock (R2-02). Both quietly turn "slow at the technical work" into a story cost, which is the one thing a teaching game should never do. Fix those two and difficulty is in good shape.

The fun risks are a mystery that resolves itself (R2-04) and a talk-down the player can't lose (R2-15). In each, the player is being *told* the answer instead of working it out. Neither needs to get harder, only more active.

**Does the player always know what to do next?**

- **m09:** yes on the critical path. The aims are staged, the drop terminal is a fixed home, and every flag has a HaX line saying what it bought. The gaps are the purge (what is at risk, and where) and the Recruiter's offer (the player needs to know, before choosing, what the cross-reference would cost them instead). Both are HaX-hub lines.
- **m10:** mostly. The aims are clear, and the one-room silent gallery with a last line naming the door is well judged. Gaps: why to visit the plant (R2-13); where the raid team is and how to accuse (R2-22); and the core, where the player must know that flags 3–4 come *before* the conversation and that he will wait (R2-02). A first-time player who walks up to a seated villain will try to talk first. The core needs an obvious first action (the host terminal lit, HaX: "Get on his box. He'll keep.").
- **Join** is signposted better than anything else in the plan.
