## 2. Verdict

**Revise, then round 2.** The draft is careful and largely sound: every cost is said before the pick by someone with a stake, imports never gate, the five full scenes ask the right questions for the season, and the endings are six distinct positions that keep to the user's decisions. The cold ending is signposted without becoming a trap. The core's four menus are where they belong.

Three things hold it back. First, the database choice, the season's teaching spine, stacks every public harm on one side, so disclose reads as the right answer and wipe as the worst success; its timescale is also wrong for a game that teaches disclosure (F-04, F-05, F-06). Second, the night's free task (stop the head start) quietly removes one side of five other dilemmas, and recruited Victoria's trust option is a hidden-cost trap on a menu (F-02, F-03). Third, Act 2 is overloaded with second decisions that pay in the same small coin (one guard, one camera) and land in the one room where nobody can say why (F-01, F-08, F-09). Cut three sub-decisions (§6), give the gallery's guards the lines that attribute what the player did earlier, and rebalance the database, and this is a strong stage 3.

Of the 19 listed choices, nine are moral dilemmas; the rest are puzzles, stances, tasks or a hub, and the file should label them so (§3).

## 4. The endings

**Six distinct positions.** Yes. Arrest is justice in public; surrender is a pragmatic deal that buys cells with silence; justified lethal is protective force under uncertainty; executed is revenge; Scorched Earth is "nobody should hold this"; Join is agreement with him. F13 is what makes arrest and surrender different positions rather than a better and a worse result, and Netherton's one-breath line before the menu (§6.6) states the trade in the fiction. HaX's last words separate them again at 06:00. No change needed to the set.

**The cold ending: signposting.** It avoids the trap. The button names the act and his posture, sits last, shares no word with "[Fire.]", and needs a second pick after a narrator warning that says "execution". It can't be reached by a misclick, a failed talk-down or an import. "Lower the weapon" removing it is the right call: a player who has already looked at it and put it down shouldn't be offered it again.

**The cold ending: preaching.** Mostly avoided, because the weight is carried by people who have to answer for it (HaX's silence, Netherton refusing to sign, Quintero taking the weapon) and not by the shot. Two things tip it towards a sermon: the narrator's epigram at 06:00 ("Most of it is still a lie. The paragraph about the man you shot isn't."), which comments on the act instead of showing it, and the stacking of five separate condemnations before the card adds a sixth ("shot unarmed"). See M-06.

**Join: chilling?** Close. "Thank you, Agent." and nothing else is right; walking out with him is right; HaX's unanswered designation at her desk is the coldest image in the file. Two weaknesses. First, the casualty figure is said by the narrator at step 1 and again at step 2, then shown on the projection: by the third time it reads as the game telling the player off (M-07). Second, nothing in the scene gives a player a reason to press it beyond his speech. Join is strongest as a position if it is most tempting to the player who lost the argument (0–1 points): one line from him at step 1 on a low count ("You couldn't answer me. Then let it be you.") ties Join to the debate the player just had.

**The database: a real argument about disclosure?** Not yet. This is the most important finding in the review (F-04). As written, disclose costs SAFETYNET two operations and buys the public patches, and secure buys SAFETYNET operations and costs the public its patches. Every cost to the public sits on one side. A player who cares about people will read disclose as the right answer, and "secure" as the service protecting itself, which is the villain's claim conceded by the menu. The real disclosure argument has public costs on both sides: some flaws (water controllers, old dispatch software) can't be patched quickly or at all, and sending a full map to hundreds of vendors at once tells every leaky vendor and every buyer where to hit before fixes exist. Scorched Earth then looks like the worst success on every count (no fixes, he escapes, suspension) with a gain that is only philosophical (F-06). And the timescale ("first patches within the hour", dispatch fixed "by seven") teaches students something false about coordinated disclosure (F-05).

## 5. Findings

No blockers. Nine majors, eleven minors.

### Majors

**F-01 (major). The gallery is where Act 2's choices land, and nobody there can tell the player why.** §3.4 "The gallery camera", §3.3 (Imran's friend on the gallery shift), §3.2 (Ghost's window or blind), §3.1 (Nightshade's release), §3.5 HaX's jam. At least five earlier choices pay out in the gallery: the camera blinded in the power room, one guard fewer because Imran rang his friend, Ghost's guard-change window, a taken-in Nightshade's manual release, and the trusted-Victoria alarm. The gallery is also the one room where HaX is jammed and there is no music. A player who meets one guard fewer and no camera alerts has no way to know that two choices forty minutes apart caused it, so those choices are felt as "the gallery was easy" and nothing else. Rule 1.3 ("felt in play") is met mechanically and missed in experience. *Fix:* give each carried effect a tell in the room, voiced by the gallery's guards since HaX can't speak. Guard barks are the right vehicle: "Camera's dead again. Third time this month." (power room), "Where's Priya? She was on till six." with her mug and coat at an empty post (Imran's call), "Change-over. Two minutes." (Ghost's window), Nightshade's voice on the lock intercom (his release). One bark per effect, no explanation. Stage 4 should cost the barks; they are cheap.

**F-02 (major). Stopping the head start is free, and being free it empties five other choices.** §4 C9 ("These are tasks, not dilemmas: each lowers the night's cost and has no price of its own"), §3.2 bait, §3.5 every gallery branch, §12 `head_start_out`. Most gallery costs are "Zero Day's order stands", and Ghost's bait costs "the head start goes to the buyers". Both are void once C9 is done. A competent player does C9 (HaX warns twice and it costs nothing), so they meet the gallery with one side of each dilemma already zeroed: arresting escaped Victoria and refusing Vos become free, and Ghost's bait beats talking Ghost out (it adds a cell and loses nothing). The draft calls this "the console task paying back", but the effect is that the careful player gets the shallowest version of the night's moral choices. *Fix (preferred):* the console has time to stop **one** share, not both: Zero Day's or Ghost's buyers'. HaX: "I can kill one feed before four. Not both. Pick." That turns a free task into a real choice that feeds both scenes (bait Ghost and stop Ghost's share here, or talk Ghost out and stop Zero Day's), and keeps every gallery cost alive on one side. *Alternative:* keep C9 whole but give it a cost in time that the whodunit feels (Kowalczyk goes ahead unwatched while the player is at the console; arc §6.3 already has her going ahead in the server hall).

**F-03 (major). Recruited Victoria's "[Open it.]" is a hidden-cost trust test on a menu, and it is the wrong answer every time.** §3.5 Recruited, rule 1.2, §13 D5. Rule 1.2 allows one trust test whose cost lands after the pick, and §3.1 says the Nightshade payment is "the one place rule 1.2's exception is used", built as an action so that "there is no '[Trust him]' button competing with a '[Check it]' button". The recruited-Victoria menu is exactly that: "[Open it.]" next to a confront button. Trusting her always ends the same way (she sells the raid: alarm, reinforcements, Zero Day's order stands, her watch is over). Compared with "[No. Stand away from it.]", which keeps the watch and the tap, trust gains only the time saved at the lock. It is dominated, and on replay it is a known trap. *Fix:* make her choice depend on the night, so trusting her is a judgement the player can make from evidence. She goes with whoever is winning: if the head start is already stopped (or, after F-02, if Zero Day's share is stopped), Zero Day has nothing left to pay her and she opens the lock and keeps both operations; if it is live, she sells. HaX's last line before the jam gives the rule: "Sterling goes with whoever's winning. Make sure it's us before you hand her anything." Now trust can be right, confront is the safe check, and refuse is the cautious path.

**F-04 (major). The database choice puts every cost to the public on one side.** §6.5 table, §9 question 1. Secure costs the public (pumps still live next year) and buys SAFETYNET operations; disclose costs SAFETYNET operations and buys the public patches. A player who cares about people sees disclose as right and secure as the service protecting itself, which concedes his second claim in the menu's own framing. That is not the real disclosure argument, and the game's teaching point is weaker for it. The real argument has public harm on both sides: some flaws can't be patched fast or at all (old water controllers, dispatch software out of support), and sending a full map to hundreds of vendors at once tells every buyer with a contact at a leaky vendor where to hit before fixes exist. *Fix:* give secure a public-safety reason, said by Netherton because it is his argument to make: "Half of these can't be patched this year. The water controllers can't be patched at all. Send those out and you've told every buyer where to hit." Then disclose's 06:00 beat carries one public cost as well as the patch count: HaX, "Three utilities went to manual at five. Someone read the vendor list." Secure keeps its present cost (the pumps). Now each option has a cost to strangers, and the player is weighing harms, not the service against the public.

**F-05 (major). The disclosure timescale is wrong, in a game meant to teach it.** §6.5 disclose gain ("the first patches land within the hour"), §8.2 database row, §10 human line 1 ("answered every call by seven, after the first patch"). Coordinated disclosure works over weeks to months; vendors triage, write, test and ship. A patch within the hour, and eleven counties' dispatch fixed by 07:00, tells students disclosure is instant and painless, which also tips F-04 further. *Fix:* keep the beat, change the numbers. At 06:00: "Forty vendors have acknowledged. Two had fixes on the shelf and shipped them." The card's dispatch line becomes a later date: "Dispatch in eleven counties: patched on day nine." (on `disclosed`) / "still dropping calls. Day thirteen." (otherwise). The contrast still lands, and it is true to how it works.

**F-06 (major). Wipe both is dominated in play, and the escape is not said before the pick.** §6.1 HaX's purge-rule line, §6.5 wipe row, §8.1. Its costs are concrete and severe (no fixes, he walks free, the player is suspended, step 5 is gone); its gain is a sentence ("Then nobody has it"). The escape, the biggest cost, is signposted only by "I can't see what it does to the doors", said once at flag 4, several minutes and two menus earlier. A player who picks wipe on principle and then loses Tesseract and the end-for-him choice will feel tricked, which is the trap the brief forbids for the non-success path and should apply here too. *Fix:* (1) say the escape plainly at the menu. HaX, with the menu open: "If both copies die, this room purges itself. Doors, lights, everything. You won't have him after." (2) Give wipe one gain the player sees at 06:00, so "nobody holds the map" is felt: Netherton, "Two cells paid for early access tonight. They paid for nothing. Nobody can sell this again, us included." That makes it a position (abolish the stockpile, at your own cost) rather than the worst success.

**F-07 (major). The gallery-camera switch, if it is an object on the panel, makes "his hands" and "tell him" dominant.** §3.4 "The gallery camera", §14 stages 4–5 ("the panel's gallery-camera switch as an object"). The draft says Voltage won't touch the switch and Wendell doesn't know what it is, but nothing stops the player throwing it after either has made the cut. Then letting Voltage work (fast, a record answer, the inquiry ask) and telling Wendell (he cuts it, then rings Dale) cost nothing over the by-hand route, and D4 and D11 are undone. *Fix:* the camera is only reachable inside the by-hand switching task (the shutter and the camera are on the same isolator, and the man doing the cut has his hand on it: Voltage, "Leave that one. I'm not giving them a reason."). Stage 5 needs this as a rule, not an object.

**F-08 (major). Too many choices pay in the same small coin: one guard, one camera, one alert.** §3.1 (arrested Nightshade refused: one camera alert; silent: one more guard), §3.2 (Ghost's keys: a camera alert; Ghost's blind), §3.3 (Imran's friend: one guard fewer), §3.4 (Dale: one alert; gallery camera), §5 (wrong Arakawa: one more guard). At least eight options are worth plus or minus one guard or one camera. In a game with stealth, knock-outs and cloned cards, one guard is noise: the player can't tell a choice's guard from the bunker's guards, and the costs feel the same whichever moral choice produced them. *Fix:* keep at most three of these as guard/camera effects, each with a tell (F-01); move the rest onto people. Candidates: Dale's call costs a line from Wendell, not an alert (§6 below); the arrested-Nightshade refusal's camera becomes Quintero's line at the lock alone (she already has one); Ghost's keys-used camera becomes a Ghost text mid-scene ("Left at the stairs. I can see you, and so can they.") instead of an alert.

**F-09 (major). The load is about four times a shipped mission's, in about 1.5 times the time.** §2, §3, §6; arc §2. m01 and m05 each have one big menu at the confrontation plus one or two mid-mission choices; m07 has one team assignment read back everywhere. A rich m09 save in m10 meets about 25 consequential picks (C2's two, C4's two, C5's one or two, C6's two, C8, C9, C10, C11, C12 and a recall, C13's one or two, three exchanges, C15, C16, C17 with a sub-beat, C18), most with three or four outcomes, in 105 minutes that are also carrying four flags and a whodunit. The finale has earned more, and the core's four menus are right where they are. The problem is Act 2, where each full scene stacks a second decision on the first inside a seven-minute budget, and several of those second decisions pay only in the credits. A player will not follow which of 25 picks made which of 40 credit clauses. *Fix:* the cuts and merges in §6, which remove four sub-decisions from Act 2 without losing a felt carry-over.
